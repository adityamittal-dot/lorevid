# Research — Your Life as a Royal Falconer in a Medieval Court

Sources used: birdspot.co.uk, hiddenhistoryfacts.com, en.wikisource.org, medievalists.net, en.wikipedia.org, englishhistoryauthors.blogspot.com, falconryadvice.com, medievalchronicles.com, britannica.com, donwinn.blog.

1. The English "Boke of Seynt Albans" (printed 1486) assigned each social rank a specific hawk species one was legally entitled to fly: emperor — eagle; king — gyrfalcon; prince — falcon gentle; duke — falcon of the loch; earl — peregrine; baron — bustard; knight — sacre; esquire — lanere; lady — merlin; yeoman — goshawk; poor man — tercel; priest — sparrowhawk; servant or knave — kestrel. — https://www.birdspot.co.uk/culture/the-history-of-falconry
2. Flying a hawk above one's allotted rank was treated as an offence against the social order the book set out, though how strictly courts enforced it is unclear (debated). — https://www.birdspot.co.uk/culture/the-history-of-falconry
3. Holy Roman Emperor Frederick II's own decree valued a trained peregrine falcon at one thousand marks, roughly a high-ranking knight's entire yearly income. — https://www.hiddenhistoryfacts.com/article/medieval-falconrys-high-value
4. England's King Henry III paid twenty pounds for a single gyrfalcon in the year twelve fifty, enough silver to equip about a dozen knights. — https://www.hiddenhistoryfacts.com/article/medieval-falconrys-high-value
5. In twelve seventy-six the King of Norway sent King Edward the First of England eight grey and three white gyrfalcons as a diplomatic gift sealing peace between the two crowns. — https://www.hiddenhistoryfacts.com/article/medieval-falconrys-high-value
6. Holding the captured son of the Duke of Burgundy for ransom in the late thirteen-hundreds, the Ottoman Sultan Bayezid turned down two hundred thousand gold ducats and instead demanded twelve white gyrfalcons. — https://www.hiddenhistoryfacts.com/article/medieval-falconrys-high-value
7. Medieval Welsh law fixed the price of a falcon's nest at six score pence, and a young falcon still in its first mew at three score pence. — https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Welsh_Medieval_Law.djvu/326
8. In fifteenth-century Wartenstein in Austria, a peasant caught disturbing a wild falcon's nest could be blinded or have his property confiscated. — https://www.medievalists.net/2024/12/stealing-horses-losing-feet-crusader-animal-laws/
9. Elsewhere, destroying a falcon's egg could bring a year's imprisonment, and a poacher who took a wild falcon risked having his own eyes put out. — https://www.medievalists.net/2024/12/stealing-horses-losing-feet-crusader-animal-laws/
10. In the Crusader states, stealing a mature hawk or falcon carried a fine of one hundred besants. — https://www.medievalists.net/2024/12/stealing-horses-losing-feet-crusader-animal-laws/
11. The word "mews" comes from the French muer, meaning to moult; the buildings were named for housing hawks through their annual moult, not, as later English usage suggests, for stabling horses. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mews_(falconry)
12. A well-built mews had at least one window to let in a little light, a door wide enough for a falconer to pass through with a bird still on his fist, and a floor of gravel or coarse sand changed at regular intervals. — https://englishhistoryauthors.blogspot.com/2015/07/the-medieval-mews.html
13. Inside, perches of different heights suited different birds, and hawks stood hooded and tethered by their jesses in near silence, with a bathing place kept close by. — https://englishhistoryauthors.blogspot.com/2015/07/the-medieval-mews.html
14. A falconer weighed his birds daily, since a hawk's willingness to hunt depended on being kept at a precise weight — too heavy and it ignored the lure, too light and it grew weak. — https://falconryadvice.com/birds/hawks/what-do-falconers-feed-their-hawks/
15. A hawk was fed meat with the fur or feathers still attached, called a casting, so the indigestible matter could be coughed back up as a pellet and keep its digestive tract clean. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pellet_(ornithology)
16. A broken flight feather was repaired by "imping" — splicing in a matching feather saved from a moulted or dead bird so the hawk's flight was not grounded while a new one grew in. — https://falconryadvice.com/equipment/falconry-equipment-list/
17. Frederick the Second spent some thirty years compiling his six-book treatise "On the Art of Hunting with Birds," finished in the twelve-forties and grounded in his own experiments, including covering a vulture's eyes to test whether it hunted by smell. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_arte_venandi_cum_avibus
18. The office of royal falconer was a real position at court, its holder seated fourth at the king's own table after the sovereign — a mark of daily closeness to power rare for a household servant. — https://www.medievalchronicles.com/medieval-life/medieval-games/falconry/
19. Larger royal households additionally kept a Master of the Mews to oversee the birds' daily training and housing, so the care of hawks could form its own small department within the court (how sharply this role was divided from the falconer's in smaller households is debated). — https://www.medievalchronicles.com/medieval-life/medieval-games/falconry/
20. Falconry reached medieval Europe partly through Crusaders and merchants who encountered the sport in the Middle East and brought trained birds and falconers home with them. — https://www.britannica.com/sports/falconry
21. Falcons served as tools of diplomacy across medieval Europe and the Middle East and were, on at least one recorded occasion, accepted as a ransom payment rather than gold. — https://www.britannica.com/sports/falconry
22. A well-trained goshawk could put meat on a household's table, so among the lesser gentry falconry was as much practical food-getting as sport, even as the cost of training and equipping a hawk kept it an upper-class pursuit overall. — https://donwinn.blog/2013/11/07/falconry-part-one-medieval-and-modern-falconry/
23. The Book of Saint Albans (1486), also called the Book of Hawking, Hunting and Blasing of Arms, paired its hawk-and-rank hierarchy with practical instruction on feeding birds and treating their illnesses. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Saint_Albans
24. Standard falconry equipment — the leather gauntlet, the soft hood with its small beak and nostril openings, the leather jesses through the bird's anklets, and small bronze or silver bells on the legs so a lost hawk could be heard and found — was already in use by the medieval period and is essentially unchanged today. — https://falconryadvice.com/equipment/falconry-equipment-list/
