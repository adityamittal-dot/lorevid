# Draft 1 — Your Life as a Royal Falconer in a Medieval Court

## Chapter 1: The Chosen Hand
The steward's bell rings across the muddy square, and every boy in the village lines up, because today the old falconer chooses his last apprentice.
You have never held anything smaller than a plow handle, yet you step forward before your knees can talk you out of it.
The falconer studies your hands, not your face, turning your palms up to the grey morning light without a single word.
"Steady hands," he says at last, "and a quiet voice, that is all a hawk ever asks of a man."
Your mother presses a heel of bread into your fist at the cart, her eyes wet, her voice carefully calm.
Your father says nothing, only rests one rough hand on your shoulder, the weight of it staying with you all the way to the castle.
Inside the bailey wall, the mews stands apart from the kitchens and stables, a low stone building with a single narrow window.
The falconer, whom you will soon call master, warns you before you even cross the threshold never to raise your voice inside these walls.
You step into a hush deeper than any church, hooded birds standing silent on their perches, waiting, breathing, listening for footsteps.
At the far perch sits a white gyrfalcon, the king's own bird, worth by royal decree more than a knight earns in a year.
Your master tells you plainly that a law in this kingdom fixes which bird a man may fly by his rank alone.
A servant caught flying a hawk above his station, he says, answers for it, and a stolen falcon's nest carries its own harsh price.
That night you lie on a straw pallet in the mews loft, listening to feathers shift in the dark, wondering if you are equal to any of it.

## Chapter 2: The Silent Mews
Your master teaches you first to walk, not to fly a bird, since a startled hawk remembers every clumsy footstep for its whole life.
You learn to lace the soft leather hood over dark, watchful eyes, easing the little bird from bright fear into patient darkness.
The jesses go through the anklets next, thin leather straps your master ties with fingers steadier than any you have ever seen.
Small bronze bells ring faintly at the ankles, so that if the wind ever carries a hawk away, her voice can still be found.
Every morning before the sun clears the wall, you set each bird on the scale, because a hawk too full will not hunt for you.
A hawk too light grows weak and sullen, your master warns, so the balance between hunger and strength becomes your whole daily art.
You feed the birds meat still wrapped in fur or feather, a rough casting they cough back up to keep their insides clean.
When a flight feather snaps, you learn to splice in another, imping the break so smoothly the bird barely feels the loss.
The building itself, your master tells you, takes its name from the French word for moulting, muer, the season when every hawk hides away to change.
Each spring the mews grows quieter still, curtained and dim, while the birds shed old feathers no one is allowed to disturb.
You learn to lead a hawk to water at dusk, watching her shake and settle, feathers gleaming like wet slate in the last light.
Months pass this way, hands roughened by leather and blood, patience stitched into you one silent, repeated morning at a time.
One grey dawn your master finally sets the gyrfalcon on your own gloved fist, and for a moment neither of you breathes.

## Chapter 3: The King's Favor
The king rides out one autumn morning to fly his gyrfalcon, and your master, testing you, sends you forward in his place.
Your hands shake lacing the jesses before the whole hunting party, every noble eye weighing whether a village boy deserves this bird.
You lift your fist, slip the hood, and the gyrfalcon rises into a sky the color of a knife blade, and everyone falls silent.
She climbs and climbs until she is only a pale mark against the clouds, then folds and drops like something falling from heaven.
The quarry never has a chance, and when she returns to your glove, the king himself laughs aloud in open delight.
From that day the king asks for you by name, and a seat near the high table opens for a falconer for the first time in years.
You eat that night closer to the king than half the lords in the hall, and you do not taste a single bite of it.
Not everyone welcomes a plowman's son so near the throne, and you feel one courtier's stare like a hand pressed flat against your back.
That courtier, a knight named for a hawk he is not permitted to fly, begins asking loudly what a servant knows that lords do not.
Your master warns you quietly that favor at court is a hooded thing too, calm until the moment someone slips its jesses.
Still, winter turns to spring turns to summer, and the gyrfalcon knows your whistle now before she knows your master's.
You write nothing down, but every fact about her moods lives now in your hands the way scripture lives in a priest's mouth.
Then comes the hunt at Ashden Wood, where a sudden storm will take from you the one thing you have learned to love most.

## Chapter 4: The Lost Hawk
Thunder rolls in from the west faster than anyone reads it, and the gyrfalcon bolts high after a heron, chasing straight into the storm.
You call and call, swinging the lure until your arm burns, but the wind tears her bells away from you like a stolen name.
Riders turn back to the castle before dark, and you are left alone at the treeline, refusing to leave without her.
The jealous courtier reminds anyone who will listen that losing the king's own bird is no small servant's mistake to forgive.
You remember, cold to the bone, that a stolen or lost falcon can cost a man his eyes in the old law books.
All night you walk the wet fields, calling into the dark for a bird who has no name, only the sound your voice makes for her.
Near dawn you hear it, faint and furious, bronze bells ringing from a low branch heavy with rain and one soaked, furious falcon.
She is tangled by her own jesses in the branches, exhausted, wings dragging, too proud even now to call out for help.
You climb without thinking of the drop, murmuring the same low words you have said to her every morning for two years.
When your glove finally reaches her, she steps onto it shaking, and something in your chest unclenches for the first time since the storm broke.
You carry her home wrapped inside your own cloak, her heartbeat fast and small against your ribs the whole walk back.
The king asks for the truth in front of the whole hall, and your master answers before you can, refusing to let the blame land on you.
In the end it is not punishment that finds you, but the king's own relief, plain and unguarded, at seeing his falcon safe.

## Chapter 5: Seasons of the Mews
Years turn quietly after that, the courtier's whispers fading the way most storms do once the sky finally clears for good.
You marry, in time, a laundress from the castle village, and raise a son who follows you into the mews before he can properly walk.
Your master grows old and stooped, and one spring morning he sets his own worn glove into your hands without a word of ceremony.
You become the one new boys are afraid of on their first morning, though you never once raise your voice inside these walls.
The gyrfalcon herself grows old beside you, her feathers pale with age, content now to sit the whole afternoon on your fist.
You teach your son, as you were taught, to walk quietly first, to lace a hood gently, to weigh hunger against strength every morning.
He asks you once why you never wanted a lord's title, only ever a bird and a bell and a low stone room.
You tell him that some men are given a throne, and some are simply given the sky, and you were given the sky.
On the evening you finally set down your own glove for the last time, the mews is quieter than you have ever heard it.
Your son takes your place at the scale the next morning, weighing the old gyrfalcon's own daughter as gently as you once weighed her mother.
You sit outside in the last low sun, listening to bells you no longer need to see to recognize, each one a small, familiar name.
Somewhere above the wall a young hawk circles once, twice, then folds her wings and comes home to a hand that is not yours anymore.
You close your eyes to the same sound you fell asleep to as a boy in this stone room, and you are, at last, still content.
