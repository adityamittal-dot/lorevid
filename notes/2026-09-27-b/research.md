# Research — Your Life as a Royal Physician in the Joseon Court

1. The Joseon dynasty ruled Korea from thirteen ninety-two to eighteen ninety-seven, and historians divide its five centuries into Early, Middle and Late Joseon periods — https://www.worldhistory.org/Early_Joseon_Period/
2. Korea achieved movable metal-type printing by fourteen-oh-three, decades before such presses reached Europe, alongside early Joseon inventions like a self-striking water clock, a public sundial and an armillary sphere — https://www.worldhistory.org/Early_Joseon_Period/
3. The royal medical bureau, Naeuiwon, had its name formally fixed in fourteen forty-three under King Sejong, staffed by sixteen physicians each called an eoui, meaning royal physician — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naeuiwon
4. Naeuiwon physicians managed the royal family's health, prescribed therapeutic diets suited to each patient's symptoms, and kept and preserved the palace's stores of medicinal herbs and minerals — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naeuiwon
5. Only a candidate who passed the uigwa, the state medical licensing examination, could serve inside the palace bureaucracy as a uigwan, a physician officer — https://www.jeehp.org/journal/view.php?doi=10.3352%2Fjeehp.2015.12.16
6. Because a male physician could not touch a royal woman's skin, doctors tied a silk thread to the wrist of a queen or consort and read her pulse through its faint vibration alone — https://www.daytranslations.com/blog/uinyeo-korean-physician/
7. When examining the queen mother, she sat behind a screen and passed through only a small bandaged patch of wrist, barely enough skin exposed for a pulse to be felt — https://www.daytranslations.com/blog/uinyeo-korean-physician/
8. Because women often refused treatment from male doctors out of modesty, sometimes dying of preventable illness, the palace trained uinyeo, female physicians who could perform pulse diagnosis, acupuncture and dispense medicine to the court's women — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uinyeo
9. Uinyeo were trained inside the palace in medicine yet were drawn from low-born or servant backgrounds, leaving them with an unusual status: skilled physicians who remained legally bound as servants (debated: sources differ on how much independence this gave them) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uinyeo
10. Heo Jun, born around fifteen thirty-nine as the son of a concubine, was barred by law from most noble careers and turned to medicine instead, becoming a court physician in his early thirties and the king's own physician by thirty-seven — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heo_Jun ; https://hekint.org/2017/01/29/heo-jun-brilliant-physician-in-16th-century-korea/
11. In fifteen ninety, Heo Jun successfully carried the crown prince through smallpox, and King Seonjo rewarded him by raising his official government rank — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heo_Jun
12. King Seonjo ordered Heo Jun in fifteen ninety-six to compile a complete medical encyclopedia; the project was nearly lost when the Japanese invasions of fifteen ninety-seven scattered the court, but was finally finished in sixteen-ten and printed in sixteen-thirteen as the twenty-five volume Donguibogam — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donguibogam
13. The Donguibogam explained treatments in Hangul, the Korean script, specifically so ordinary commoners, not only scholars trained in classical Chinese, could read and use it — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donguibogam
14. UNESCO inscribed the Donguibogam on its Memory of the World Register in two thousand nine, for its lasting historical and cultural significance — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donguibogam
15. When King Seonjo died in sixteen-oh-eight, jealous officials accused his physician Heo Jun of responsibility, and he was exiled to the countryside town of Ulju — a royal physician's fortune rose and fell with the ruler's own health — https://hekint.org/2017/01/29/heo-jun-brilliant-physician-in-16th-century-korea/
16. Seonjo's successor, King Gwanghaegun, later restored Heo Jun to office over many officials' objections, allowing him to finish his life's work — https://hekint.org/2017/01/29/heo-jun-brilliant-physician-in-16th-century-korea/
17. Outside the palace, the Hyeminseo, a public medical office founded in thirteen ninety-two and settled under that name in fourteen sixty-six, gave commoners in the capital free or low-cost diagnosis, treatment and herbal medicine — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyeminseo
18. Korean palaces were warmed by ondol, an underfloor system that drew smoke and heat from a wood fire beneath the stone floor, so that even the king's own sleeping chamber was heated from below rather than by an open hearth or a bed — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ondol ; https://contents.history.go.kr/mobile/tz/view.do?levelId=tz_b40
19. Soldiers called jeonnugun were posted through the palace grounds to call out each hour, reading the time from the water clock at Borugak, whose tower stood near the sleeping quarters of the king and his consorts — https://contents.history.go.kr/mobile/tz/view.do?levelId=tz_b40
20. Each evening the injeong bell tolled the curfew and the closing of the city gates, and each dawn the paru bell woke the capital again, shaping the entire sound of a palace night — https://contents.history.go.kr/mobile/tz/view.do?levelId=tz_b40
21. Traditional Korean medicine treated illness chiefly with herbal decoctions, acupuncture and moxibustion, the burning of dried mugwort at chosen points on the skin, a smoke and scent that would have filled any palace pharmacy — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28919589/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moxibustion
22. Restorative tonics built on ginseng became especially prized through the Joseon era, reflecting a wider social trust in slow, strengthening remedies over sudden cures — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28919589/
23. An official's rank, and so a court physician's own standing among his colleagues, was announced at a glance by his robe's color: red for the most senior ranks, blue for the middle ranks, green for the most junior — https://www.korea.net/NewsFocus/Culture/view?articleId=183565
24. Only the king wore the dragon-patterned gonryongpo robe with its winged black hat, the ikseongwan, for ordinary court business, reserving an even more elaborate ceremonial robe, the myeonbok, for coronations and royal weddings — https://www.korea.net/NewsFocus/Culture/view?articleId=183565
25. Joseon society was divided into yangban aristocrats, commoners and an enslaved class, and commoners were required to keep to plain white cotton in everyday dress, a visible marker of the kingdom's rigid hierarchy — https://www.britannica.com/topic/Joseon-dynasty ; https://www.korea.net/NewsFocus/Culture/view?articleId=183565
26. Rice, millet, barley, beans, radish and cabbage were the staple crops of the era, and every winter even common families fermented pickled cabbage and seafood, an early form of kimchi, in large ceramic jars — https://www.britannica.com/topic/Joseon-dynasty
27. Standardized copper coins finally succeeded in the sixteen-hundreds where an earlier mulberry-bark paper currency, jeohwa, had failed, and two dozen mints were eventually built across the kingdom to strike them — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_currency

Notes: the protagonist's arc draws on the documented life of the royal physician Heo Jun (rise from disqualifying birth, the crown prince's smallpox, exile after the king's death, and a great medical text left to commoners), but the finished script keeps the physician unnamed, since not every incidental scene detail is separately sourced — every factual claim used in the script traces to one of the numbered facts above.
