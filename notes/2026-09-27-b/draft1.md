# Draft 1 — Your Life as a Royal Physician in the Joseon Court

## Cold open

By sunset, if the fever will not break, the ministers outside the queen's chamber will say your hands were the ones that failed her.
But hours before the candles were ever lit for that vigil, you were only a boy again in memory, standing outside a locked examination hall.

## Chapter One: The Wrong Kind of Son

You are seven when your father tells you, gently, that the state examinations are closed to you, because your mother was never his wife.
For years every proper door in Hanyang stays shut to a name like yours, but the herb stalls near the market never once ask who your mother was.
An old physician at the Hyeminseo takes you on, teaching you to read a pulse under your fingers before you can read every character of Hangul.
The clinic smells of drying mugwort and simmering roots, and beyond its doorway, carts creak over frozen streets in the blue hour before dawn.
He shows you how ginseng steadies a failing heart, and which bitter root calms a fever without ever stopping the healing sweat beneath it.
A coughing beggar you cannot save teaches you more than any scroll: medicine that never reaches the poor is only half a medicine at all.
At sixteen you sit the uigwa, the state medical examination, while officials nearby murmur about your birth behind their folded sleeves.
You pass. Somewhere behind the murmuring, your old teacher says nothing, only touches your shoulder once, the way a father might.
But a palace summons for a boy of your birth is not a gift; it is a door that can just as easily close on your fingers.

## Chapter Two: Behind the Red Gate

Inside the palace, an elder physician warns you plainly: here, a wrong diagnosis is not a lesson, it is a life, and sometimes it is yours.
You learn the sound of the palace at night: the water clock's soft chime, a sentry calling the hour, wind moving through the eaves overhead.
Heat rises from the ondol floor beneath your sleeping mat, warmth drawn up from a hidden fire, so even winter never quite reaches your bones.
You learn your robe's very color speaks before you do: crimson for the senior ranks, blue for the middle, green for a junior physician like you.
No man may lay a bare hand on a queen or a royal consort, so you are taught instead to tie a silk thread about her wrist.
Through that trembling thread alone, you must feel a pulse and name what moves beneath skin you are never permitted to touch.
When the queen mother is unwell, she sits behind a painted screen and offers only a bandaged inch of wrist through a narrow gap in the silk.
A uinyeo, a woman trained in medicine despite her lowly birth, examines what you cannot, and reports back what her hands alone are allowed to know.
You come to depend on her steady judgment more than on any scroll, though the court records will likely never once write down her name.
Some nights you brew tonics until your sleeves smell permanently of ginseng and scorched mugwort, and you no longer notice the smell yourself.
Other nights you simply wait, listening to the curfew bell toll over Hanyang's rooftops, wondering if you will ever be trusted with more than waiting.

## Chapter Three: The Night the Thread Went Still

Then one spring the young queen falls ill with a fever that climbs faster than any tonic you know can answer.
The senior physicians argue over her chamber door in whispers, each one afraid to commit his own name to the wrong remedy.
It falls, at last, to you and the silk thread, your fingers barely steady, everyone behind you waiting to see if you will fail.
For a long moment the thread lies still against your fingers, and you cannot tell if it is your fear or her pulse that has slowed.
You call for ginseng brewed strong enough to steady a failing heart, and for cooling herbs to ease the fever without stopping her sweat.
The uinyeo lights fresh moxa at careful points along her arm, and its warm mugwort smoke curls slowly upward into the dark rafters.
Hour after hour you kneel by the screen, listening for the smallest change, while outside the water clock counts the night away without you.
Just before the paru bell rings to wake the city, the thread finally jumps beneath your fingers, quick, steady, unmistakably alive again.
You do not shout, you do not smile too widely; you simply bow your head and let the relief move through you in silence.
Word of the queen's recovery moves through the palace by morning, and for a while, even the ministers who once whispered about your birth fall quiet.

## Chapter Four: What the Throne Cannot Forgive

Years pass, and you rise, trusted now with the health of princes as well as queens, your green robe long since traded for blue.
But when the old king finally dies in his own bed one winter, grief in the palace turns quickly, as it often does, into blame.
Officials who resented your birth from the very first day now whisper that his physician, not his age, must be the true cause of his death.
You are stripped of your rank and sent from the capital to a distant countryside town, the only home you have ever truly known now behind you.
In exile you keep treating whoever finds their way to your door, because a fever does not care what a court has decided about your name.
You write down every remedy you have ever tested, every root and thread and long night, so that none of it dies quietly with you.
A new king comes to the throne, hears the truth of what happened, and sends word recalling you back to the very gates that once closed on you.

## Chapter Five: What You Leave Behind

You return older, slower on the stairs, but the palace nights still smell the same, mugwort and rain and cooling stone beneath the ondol floor.
This time you are set to a greater task: to gather everything you have learned into a single book any healer in the kingdom might use.
You write it in Hangul rather than scholars' classical script, so a village healer far from any palace could read it by their own lamp.
On the last page you do not write your name in careful, boastful characters; you simply write what the thread taught you to feel.
That fevers pass more often than they take, that a trembling pulse is not yet a lost one, and that patience is its own kind of medicine.
Long after you are gone, the book will still be read by lamplight in houses that never once had a physician of their own.
And somewhere in the dark beyond the palace walls, a thread lies quiet against a stranger's wrist, waiting only for a steady hand to listen.
