# Research — Your Life as a Maratha Cavalry Soldier (2026-09-27, RESERVE slot a)

1. Shivaji split his cavalry into two ranks: bargirs, who were issued a horse and equipment from the state's own stock, and shiledars, higher-status troopers who supplied their own horse, sword and gear in exchange for better pay — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maratha_army
2. The word "shiledar" literally means "a soldier who possesses his own sword and horse" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shiledar
3. Many shiledars belonged to the old ninety-six-clan Maratha lineages and could rise through service and valor to ranks such as havaldar, subhedar and panch-hazari — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shiledar
4. Shivaji abolished the older jagir land-grant system for military officers and paid soldiers directly in cash from the state treasury, a reform known as saranjam — https://www.britannica.com/topic/Maratha-Empire
5. Shivaji deliberately kept cavalry and infantry equipment light, favoring speed and agility over heavy armor, because his campaigns were fought in the steep, forested Western Ghats — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maratha_army
6. A Maratha horseman's usual weapons were a curved sabre, a lance, a small round shield called a dhal, and — for some troopers — a matchlock or bow, rather than heavy plate armor — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maratha_army
7. Cavalry dress was practical rather than ceremonial: tight breeches, a quilted cotton coat, a long cloth sash wound at the waist to carry the sword, and a turban fastened by passing a fold under the chin — http://www.forensicfashion.com/1674MarathaCavalry.html
8. The favored mount was the Bhimthadi, a horse bred by crossing Arabian bloodstock with hardy local Deccan ponies, prized for stamina over long, fast marches — https://horsigo.com/unveiling-the-legendary-horse-army-of-chhatrapati-shivaji-maharaj/
9. Peshwa Baji Rao I's cavalry at the Battle of Palkhed (28 February 1728) rode with a spare horse for every two men, carrying only sabre, lance, bow and shield — no baggage train slowing them down — https://www.indica.today/long-reads/battle-of-palkhed/
10. At Palkhed, Baji Rao's horsemen rode around the Nizam of Hyderabad's much larger army, cutting it off from the Godavari river — its only water source — and forced a surrender without a major pitched battle, a feat still studied as a masterclass in cavalry maneuver — https://www.theweek.in/news/defence/2026/03/28/february-28-1728-the-battle-of-manoeuvre-the-battle-of-palkhed.html
11. Shivaji's guerrilla method, called ganimi kava, avoided pitched battles in favor of fast raids on enemy supply lines and columns, followed by a swift withdrawal into fortified hill country before a counterattack could form — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ganimi_Kava
12. Mughal commanders reportedly nicknamed Shivaji "Pahadi Chuha" — the mountain rat — because his forces could vanish into the hills and were almost impossible to corner — https://www.freepressjournal.in/lifestyle/what-was-chhatrapati-shivaji-maharajs-guerrilla-warfare-ganimi-kava-strategy-that-is-used-in-military-practices-today
13. Hilltop forts such as Raigad and Pratapgad let a small garrison watch enemy movement across the plains below and gave raiding parties a safe place to withdraw to after a strike — https://www.freepressjournal.in/lifestyle/what-was-chhatrapati-shivaji-maharajs-guerrilla-warfare-ganimi-kava-strategy-that-is-used-in-military-practices-today
14. Shivaji ran an intelligence service under Bahirji Naik, said to have commanded a network of roughly three thousand agents — spies disguised as ordinary travelers, plus guides who knew every mountain path — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahirji_Naik
15. Bahirji Naik's warning of an approaching twenty-thousand-strong Mughal force let Shivaji ambush it in the narrow, forested pass of Umbarkhind in 1661, turning superior enemy numbers into a trap — https://varadabooks.com/blogs/news/bahirji-naik-the-legendary-master-spy-of-shivaji-maharaj-and-the-maratha-empire
16. Shivaji's discipline code punished soldiers who harmed women or children, looted Brahmins, or damaged farmland, even in enemy territory during a campaign — https://gacbe.ac.in/pdf/ematerial/18BDS33C-U1.pdf
17. Any war booty captured by soldiers legally belonged to the state and had to be surrendered to the treasury rather than kept personally — https://gacbe.ac.in/pdf/ematerial/18BDS33C-U1.pdf
18. Women and soldiers' families were barred from accompanying the army on the march, unlike the large camp followings that trailed some contemporary Indian armies — https://trekshitiz.com/shivaji/shivaji_maharaj_lakshar_army.php
19. Campaigns followed the dry season: armies preferred to march and raid before the monsoon turned the Deccan's rivers high and its black-cotton-soil roads too heavy for a fast-moving mounted force — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deccan_wars
20. On the march a horseman typically carried roasted gram, rice, raw sugar (gur) and flour to be baked into bread once the column halted for the night — https://en.rattibha.com/thread/1449622706823913474
21. An eighteenth-century Mughal observer, Nawab Ibrahim Khan, recorded that Maratha campaign food was plain: cakes of jowar and bajra millet, lentils and ghee, sometimes spiced with red chili — https://en.rattibha.com/thread/1449622706823913474
22. Bhakri, an unleavened flatbread made from jowar (sorghum) or bajra (pearl millet), was the everyday staple of the Deccan plateau and traveled well as a soldier's ration — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhakri
23. Pay scales varied by source and period: one detailed account puts shiledar salaries around 25–30 rupees a month against 5–10 rupees for a state-equipped bargir, while a surviving 1744–45 pay list works out closer to 27–28 rupees a month for bargirs — historians' figures do not fully agree on the exact numbers (debated) — https://perspectivia.net/servlets/MCRFileNodeServlet/pnet_derivate_00005452/shirsat_maratha.pdf
24. The Maratha state raised much of its revenue through chauth, a protection tax equal to roughly a quarter of a region's assessed land revenue, paid by neighboring territories in exchange for not being raided — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maratha_Empire
25. By the 1720s the Mughal court itself had been forced to concede chauth rights over Bengal, Bihar and Orissa to the Marathas — a sign of how far Mughal authority had eroded — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maratha_Empire
26. Village governance in the Maratha homeland rested on a patil, a hereditary headman usually of the peasant-warrior caste, assisted by a kulkarni, a record-keeper usually drawn from Brahmin families — the same rural world a cavalryman returned to between campaigns — https://hindupedia.com/en/The_Marathas
27. Many Maratha soldiers — both the mavale infantry and the shiledar cavalry — were themselves part-time farmers from the hill villages of western Maharashtra, going to war in the dry season and returning to their fields and families afterward — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maratha_(caste)
28. Under Shivaji's council of eight ministers, the Ashta Pradhan, the senapati (commander-in-chief) headed the army while a separate minister, the mantri, managed intelligence and internal security reports reaching the court — https://www.britannica.com/topic/Ashta-Pradhan
29. Shivaji was an ardent devotee of the goddess Bhavani, the Bhosale clan's family deity, and according to tradition carried a sword also named Bhavani that he credited to her blessing before battle; Marathas widely worshipped Bhavani as a protector in war — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhavani
30. Shivaji is said to have regularly sought Bhavani's blessing at her temple in Tuljapur before major campaigns, tying personal faith directly to the rhythm of going to war — https://aryaakasha.com/2021/05/14/the-sword-of-the-king-on-bhavani-and-the-famed-bhavani-tulwar-of-shivaji/
