# Your Life as a Royal Elephant Keeper in Mughal India — Draft 1

## Chapter One: The Elephant Who Would Not Eat

By the time the rains break, you will know whether the frightened grey calf tied outside your father's stable trusts you, or grieves herself to death.

It is your eighth year, in the season when Fatehpur Sikri still gleams red and new above the dust of the Agra plain.

Your father is head mahout in the emperor's stables, and tonight he has carried home a problem no rope or whip can solve.

The calf was caught after her herd was driven off; her ears are torn, her eyes rimmed white, and for three days she has refused every mouthful.

Your father crouches an arm's length away and simply waits, saying nothing, letting his own stillness become the only safe thing in her world.

He tells you an elephant remembers kindness and cruelty both, for the whole of a life that may outlast your own by decades.

You are given one task only: sit near her each evening, and speak softly, and never once raise your voice or your hand.

On the fourth night you bring a lump of jaggery on your open palm, and hold it out, and do not move, and do not hope too hard.

Her trunk uncurls, trembling, and touches your fingers first, testing, before it finally closes around the sweetness and lifts it away.

You name her Pari, which means fairy, because your mother says only a fairy could make a boy's hands stop shaking with joy.

Your father says nothing, but that night he lets you carry the lamp yourself on the walk back to your quarters near the stable wall.


But a calf who eats from your hand is not yet an elephant who will carry an empress, or a general, or the weight of the state.

## Chapter Two: The Ropes and the Rice

Your days now begin before the muezzin's call, scrubbing dried mud from Pari's hide with a rough stone brush while she leans into your arms.

Ain thirty-five of the emperor's household regulations lists exactly how much grain, ghee and sugar a royal elephant is owed each single day.

You learn to read her mood from her ears alone, from the set of her trunk, from the particular way she shifts her weight before deciding.

Your father teaches you the ankusha, the hooked goad every mahout carries, and warns you it is for guiding, never for punishing.

Pari learns twenty words from your mouth before her third year, each one paired with a gentle push and a reward of sugarcane.





At twelve, you ride Pari for the first time without a rope to hold, guided only by the pressure of your knees behind her ears.

She is patient with your mistakes in a way no teacher has ever been, forgiving a wrong signal before you have even finished giving it.

Your father grows quieter each season, his hands stiffening around the ankusha, and one evening he tells you the stables will soon be yours.

You do not yet understand what it means to be responsible for an animal who could, in a single bad hour, undo everyone's trust.

## Chapter Three: The Month of Musth

In your nineteenth year, Pari's temple begins to weep a dark, strong-smelling fluid, and your father's face goes still with old, careful fear.

This is musth, the yearly season when even the gentlest bull or cow elephant turns unpredictable, driven by something older than training.

Handlers say a chained elephant in musth understands only one voice, and you spend that whole month sleeping within earshot of her chain.

On the worst night, Pari strains against her post so hard the whole stable timber groans, and you do not move from the doorway.

You speak the same low words you spoke to a frightened calf eleven years before, and slowly, slowly, her great head lowers again.

By dawn she presses her trunk to your shoulder, gentle as ever, as though the night's violence belonged to some other, stranger elephant entirely.

Weeks later, calm again, Pari is chosen for a procession before the emperor himself, who is said to know every elephant's name by heart.

Your hands shake lacing the ceremonial cloth, dyed deep red and stitched with small mirrors that throw light across the palace courtyard.

Trumpets sound, drums beat, and Pari walks the processional road so steadily that a watching noble asks who trained such a calm animal.

You are twenty years old, standing in the dust beside four tons of trained strength, and for one bright moment the whole world watches you both.

That night your father embraces you for the first time in years, and says only, she is truly yours now, in front of the whole household.

You marry the following spring, a quiet woman named Zainab whose father keeps horses, and who is not at all frightened of your enormous friend.

But even a day so full of joy cannot erase what every mahout in the empire's stables eventually learns about the darker uses of strength.

## Chapter Four: What the Stables Also Carry

Some elephants here are trained for the emperor's harsher justice, and you have seen, once, from a careful distance, what that training is for.

You do not speak of that morning again, not to Zainab, not to your father, though you notice Pari seems gentler with you for weeks after.

In war, you learn, elephants break down fortress gates and scatter foot soldiers, clearing paths that no cavalry alone could open in time.


Your son Yusuf is born that same difficult year, small and loud, and Pari is the first creature outside your family allowed near his cradle.

You teach Yusuf, as your father taught you, that an elephant remembers kindness and cruelty both, for a life longer than any single mahout's.


Once Pari refuses, absolutely refuses, to cross a stretch of riverbank that later proves to hide a current strong enough to have drowned you both.

You learn to trust her caution as much as her strength, and never again mistake her stubbornness for simple animal foolishness.

The years arrive quietly after that: two more children, a larger house near the stable wall, grey threading through your father's beard and then your own.


You begin, without quite noticing, to walk more slowly beside her, matching a pace that used to feel impossibly patient to a restless boy.

## Chapter Five: The Long Evening

Yusuf is twelve now, the age you were when you first fed a lump of jaggery to a grieving, half-wild calf.

You watch him sit, exactly as your father once sat, an arm's length from a new and frightened young elephant, saying nothing at all.

Pari no longer marches in processions; her joints ache in the cold season, and the court has long since chosen younger, stronger animals for show.

Instead she spends her mornings in the shade of the stable's mango tree, and you spend yours sitting with your back against her warm flank.

You tell her, half joking, that you have both outlived most of the men who once called you the emperor's finest young mahout.

Some evenings Zainab brings you tea in the yard, and the three of you, woman, man and ageing elephant, watch the sky change colour together.

Your father died three winters past, in his own bed, with Pari's low, rolling call sounding once from the yard outside his window.

You still do not know how she understood, but she was the only one, that whole night, who made no sound at all until morning.

Yusuf asks you, one quiet evening, whether it is strange to love an animal more than you have loved almost any person.

You tell him honestly that it is not strange at all, only rare, and that rare things deserve to be tended with your whole life.

The empire remembers emperors, generals, and the polished chronicles written in their names, in libraries far grander than any stable.

It does not usually remember the boy who once held out his open palm and waited, patient, for a broken-hearted calf to trust him.

But somewhere in an old stable yard near Agra, a grey elephant leans her head against a greying man's shoulder, exactly as she always has.

And as the evening settles, warm and unhurried, you close your eyes against her side, and let her slow, familiar breathing carry you toward sleep.
