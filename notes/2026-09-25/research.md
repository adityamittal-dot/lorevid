# Research — Your Life as a Royal Elephant Keeper in Mughal India

Sources used (6 distinct sites): en.wikipedia.org, britannica.com, worldhistory.org, theheritagelab.in, acoup.blog, collections.vam.ac.uk

1. The Ain-i-Akbari, the administrative record of Emperor Akbar's government, is organised into five books; its Ain 35 covers the use and maintenance of artillery and the upkeep, branding and feeding of the emperor's royal horses, camels, mules and elephants — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ain-i-Akbari
2. The Ain-i-Akbari was written in Persian by Akbar's court historian Abu'l Fazl between 1589 and 1596, as the third volume of the larger Akbarnama — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ain-i-Akbari
3. Akbar first encountered elephants when he rode into Hindustan at age twelve, and the animal became what one historian calls his "spirit animal," a lifelong passion — https://www.theheritagelab.in/akbar-favourite-animals/
4. Akbar reportedly knew the names of every one of his elephants and their mahouts; his own favourite elephant was named Bal Sundar — https://www.theheritagelab.in/akbar-favourite-animals/
5. Akbar made a point of honour of learning to ride and tame even the wildest elephants, including ones in musth, reasoning that if he survived it was God's will (debated as court legend rather than verified fact) — https://www.theheritagelab.in/akbar-favourite-animals/
6. In 1561, outside the fort of Agra, Akbar mounted the royal elephant Hawa'i — reputed one of the strongest and most difficult the emperor owned — and pitted him against an equally fierce elephant, Ran Bagha — https://www.theheritagelab.in/akbar-favourite-animals/
7. A favourite one-tusked imperial elephant named Nainsukh was said to have killed the rebel commander Ali Quli Khan in battle in the 1590s — https://www.theheritagelab.in/akbar-favourite-animals/
8. In the 1590s the Adil Shah of Bijapur sent Akbar a favourite female elephant named Chanchal as a gift, said to be accustomed to a daily diet that included measures of wine, preferably Portuguese (debated — likely court anecdote, treated as such) — https://www.theheritagelab.in/akbar-favourite-animals/
9. A mahout is an elephant rider, trainer or keeper; mahouts traditionally came from families with generations of elephant-keeping experience and usually kept and worked with one elephant across its entire working life — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahout
10. The main tool of a mahout is the ankusha (or ankus), a sharp metal hook used to guide and control the elephant — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahout
11. Elephants can learn to recognise more than twenty distinct spoken commands from their mahout — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahout
12. A howdah is a carriage or seat fixed to an elephant's back, used to carry wealthy or royal riders on processions, hunts or into war, and could be elaborately decorated, even with gemstones — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howdah
13. The howdah as a fixed tower-seat did not reach India until around the twelfth century CE, likely carried there by Islamic armies from further west — https://acoup.blog/2019/07/26/collections-war-elephants-part-i-battle-pachyderms/
14. Once a year, adult male elephants enter musth, a state of heightened aggression and mating drive; historians record that handlers sometimes used diet and training to bring on musth deliberately before a battle, because the resulting aggression was militarily prized (debated practice, and dangerous for handlers) — https://acoup.blog/2019/07/26/collections-war-elephants-part-i-battle-pachyderms/
15. War elephants were generally selected for combativeness, and the ideal war elephant was judged an adult male of around forty years old — https://acoup.blog/2019/07/26/collections-war-elephants-part-i-battle-pachyderms/
16. A mature Asian male elephant stands roughly two and three-quarter metres at the shoulder and can weigh around four tons — https://acoup.blog/2019/07/26/collections-war-elephants-part-i-battle-pachyderms/
17. Historians note that the mahout, though the one person who actually controlled the elephant, was typically a lower-status specialist, often left out of the grander written accounts that instead praised the aristocratic rider on the elephant's back — https://acoup.blog/2019/07/26/collections-war-elephants-part-i-battle-pachyderms/
18. In Mughal warfare, elephants were used to break through enemy fortifications and scatter foot soldiers — https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O9532/painting-miskina/
19. Trained elephants were also used at the Mughal court to carry out state executions of prisoners; a surviving Akbarnama painting from around 1590-95 records elephants executing followers of a defeated rebel in 1567 (a real but grim historical practice, to be treated soberly and briefly, never graphically) — https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O9532/painting-miskina/
20. The Akbarnama, the official chronicle of Akbar's reign, was written in Persian by Abu'l Fazl between 1590 and 1596 and illustrated by around forty-nine different court painters — https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O9532/painting-miskina/
21. Akbar was born in 1542 and reigned as Mughal emperor from 1556 until 1605, extending Mughal rule over most of the Indian subcontinent — https://www.britannica.com/biography/Akbar
22. Akbar built a new capital, Fatehpur Sikri, near Agra; building began in 1570 and the city was largely abandoned by 1586, and it blends Hindu and Muslim architectural styles — https://www.britannica.com/biography/Akbar
23. Every morning at dawn, Akbar stood at an open window so ordinary people could see him and bring their complaints, a public ritual foreign observers specifically noted — https://www.britannica.com/biography/Akbar
24. Akbar's court supported large state workshops producing fine textiles and ornaments, and gave liberal patronage to painters, poets and musicians — https://www.britannica.com/biography/Akbar
25. In ancient and medieval Indian warfare, an army was traditionally organised into four arms — infantry, cavalry, chariots and elephants — with the elephant corps holding a place of particular prestige even after chariots fell out of use — https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1241/elephants-in-ancient-indian-warfare/
26. Each kingdom's elephant corps had its own commander; the title for an elephant driver in ancient Indian sources was ankushadhara, "holder of the hook" — https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1241/elephants-in-ancient-indian-warfare/
27. On the march, elephants were used to clear paths, ford rivers, guard an army's flanks and rear, and batter down fortress walls — https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1241/elephants-in-ancient-indian-warfare/
28. Despite careful training, an elephant's temperament remained unpredictable in battle; a wounded or panicked elephant could trample its own side's soldiers and turn the course of a fight — a recurring judgement across multiple ancient and medieval battle accounts (debated as to frequency, but attested repeatedly) — https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1241/elephants-in-ancient-indian-warfare/
29. The Turkic and later the Mughal rulers of India adopted the long-standing Indian practice of using elephants in their armies once they had established their own kingdoms there — https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1241/elephants-in-ancient-indian-warfare/
30. Elephants were also prized purely as gifts and symbols of tributary loyalty between rulers, exchanged as diplomatic presents rather than only used in war — https://www.theheritagelab.in/akbar-favourite-animals/

## Notes on debated points
- The specific anecdotes about Akbar's favourite elephants (Bal Sundar, Hawa'i, Nainsukh, Chanchal) come from court-chronicle-derived popular history rather than a primary administrative source, so they are framed in the script as stories told about the emperor, not flatly asserted fact.
- Deliberately inducing musth before battle, and the frequency of elephants panicking and trampling their own troops, are both practices historians describe as attested but not universal; marked (debated) in the script where used.
- Elephant executions are historically real but are handled in the script with a single sober, non-graphic sentence, never described in detail, per WRITER.md's safety rules.
