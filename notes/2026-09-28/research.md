1. Roman soldiers were legally forbidden to marry for over two centuries, from Augustus until Emperor Septimius Severus lifted the ban around 197-211 AD — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_military_diploma
2. If a man was already married when he enlisted, Roman law declared the marriage void the moment he took the oath — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_military_diploma
3. Despite the ban, soldiers routinely formed stable de facto unions (contubernium) with local women, who were called "wife" in everyday life but had no legal standing as one — https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/housesteads-roman-fort-hadrians-wall/history/research/
4. Camp followers — unofficial wives, children, traders, craftspeople and slaves — lived not inside the fort but in a civilian settlement called a vicus just outside its walls — https://www.nationalgeographic.com/premium/article/vindolanda-roman-fort-england
5. Vicus buildings were long "strip houses" of timber and wattle-and-daub with thatched or tiled roofs, a shop or workshop at the street front and living quarters behind — https://limeseum.de/en/the-roman-park/cavalry-fort-and-vicus
6. At Housesteads on Hadrian's Wall, unofficial wives and children likely lived in the vicus throughout the fort's first seventy years, while rank-and-file marriage was still illegal — https://ilestours.co.uk/the-vicus-settlement-of-housesteads-fort/
7. The Vindolanda tablets, thin wooden leaf letters excavated from 1973 onward at a fort in Northumberland near Hadrian's Wall, form the earliest surviving handwritten archive in British history — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vindolanda_tablets
8. The most famous Vindolanda tablet is a birthday party invitation written around AD 100 by Claudia Severa to Sulpicia Lepidina, wife of the fort commander Flavius Cerialis — the earliest known handwriting by a woman in Latin — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claudia_Severa
9. The invitation reads in part: "I give you a warm invitation to make sure that you come to us, to make the day more enjoyable for me by your arrival, if you are present... Farewell, sister, my dearest soul" — https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/inscriptions/TabVindol291
10. Another Vindolanda tablet lists a parcel sent to a soldier: "socks... two pairs of sandals and two pairs of underpants," confirming soldiers wore socks with sandals in Britain's cold — https://www.mentalfloss.com/science/archaeology/vindolanda-tablets-facts
11. Wall garrisons wrote home begging for warm subuclae (vests) and thick abollae (cloaks) against the cold and wet, and by the end of the 2nd century swapped open caligae boots for enclosed carbatinae — https://www.mentalfloss.com/science/archaeology/vindolanda-tablets-facts
12. Vindolanda's tablets record 46 different foodstuffs supplied to the garrison, including bacon, ham, venison, chicken, oysters, apples, eggs, honey, Celtic beer and wine — https://europe.factsanddetails.com/article/entry-1104.html
13. In one letter, decurion Masculus asked commander Flavius Cerialis for the next day's orders and added a request to send more beer, since the garrison had drunk its entire stock — https://www.nationalgeographic.com/premium/article/vindolanda-roman-fort-england
14. Auxiliary infantrymen — the non-citizen frontier troops who garrisoned forts like Vindolanda — earned roughly 100-150 denarii a year depending on rank, well under a legionary's pay, with much of it withheld automatically to cover food, clothing and gear — https://www.unrv.com/military/pay-and-rewards.php
15. Soldiers' wages, once spent, fed the vicus economy directly: their money went on food, clothing, tools, services and entertainment in the civilian settlement outside the walls — https://www.roman-britain.co.uk/places/ardotalia-vicus/
16. After 25 years of service, an honourably discharged auxiliary received a bronze military diploma granting him Roman citizenship and, crucially, the retroactive right of conubium — legal marriage — with his existing partner — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_military_diploma
17. Until about 140 AD, children born to a soldier during his service could also gain citizenship through his father's diploma, though his partner was only recognized as his lawful wife, not made a citizen in her own right by it — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_military_diploma
18. Frontier populations in Roman Britain had markedly harsher demographics than the empire's core: skeletal evidence suggests frontier women lived to an average of about 28 years and men to about 36 (debated, as sample sizes are small) — https://www.history-channel.org/disease-plagues-and-28-year-lifespans-in-roman-britain/
19. Roman demographic modelling suggests between a quarter and a third of infants died in their first year, and as many as half of all children never reached adulthood — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9973969/
20. Childbirth was attended by midwives who combined practical technique with herbal remedies and religious ritual, since there was no way to treat hemorrhage, infection or obstructed labour — https://www.roman-britain.org/health-disease-and-medicine/
21. Every Roman household, however humble, kept a small shrine called a lararium where the family prayed each morning to the Lares, guardian spirits of the home, with extra offerings at family festivals — https://www.britannica.com/topic/Lar-Roman-deities
22. On the frontier, soldiers also raised altars to the discipline-goddess Disciplina and to local deities fused with Roman gods, such as Mars Thincsus, found at forts including Housesteads and Chesters — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaisiagae
23. Hadrian's Wall was built to hold back the Picts and Caledonians of the north; the tribes could mount devastating raids but lacked the numbers to overrun a well-garrisoned frontier, and shortly after the Wall's completion they did raid the area around it — https://brewminate.com/hadrians-wall-protecting-southern-britannia-from-northern-pictish-tribes/
24. Milecastles stood roughly every Roman mile along the Wall with two signal turrets between each, while larger forts such as Vindolanda and Housesteads, holding hundreds of troops, anchored the strategic points — https://www.historyskills.com/classroom/ancient-history/hadrian-s-wall/
25. Historians debate exactly how common and stable these unofficial soldier marriages really were before Severus's reform, and some argue the ban was inconsistently enforced well before it was formally lifted (debated) — https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2002/2002.06.41/
26. Vindolanda's garrison in this period included the Ninth Cohort of Batavians, a roughly thousand-strong mixed infantry-and-cavalry unit recruited from the Rhine mouth near modern Nijmegen, whose prefect Flavius Cerialis lived there with his family — https://www.heritagedaily.com/2023/07/evidence-of-the-9th-cohort-of-batavians-among-new-finds-at-roman-vindolanda/147997
