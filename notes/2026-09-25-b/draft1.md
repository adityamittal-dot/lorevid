# Draft 1 — Your Life as a Temple Dancer in the Chola Empire

## Chapter One: The Water Before Dawn

Today you stop being only your mother's daughter, and by the time the temple's bronze doors swing open, everyone in this river town will know your new name.

You stand ankle deep in the temple tank before sunrise, and your mother combs river mud from your hair with fingers that will not stop trembling.

She was a dancer here too, once, and her mother before her, so this morning has been coming since before you could walk or speak.

The priest ties a thread of raw cotton around your wrist and says a word in old Tamil you do not yet understand, but you feel its weight settle onto you.

Inside, the great tower rises so high above you that craning your neck to see its top makes you dizzy and strangely, quietly proud.

Oil lamps gutter along the stone walls, and the smell of crushed jasmine and warm ghee fills your nose before your eyes have even adjusted to the dark.

An old woman presses a string of temple jasmine into your palm and tells you, not unkindly, that your knees will ache before your feet ever learn grace.

You do not know yet what nine years of mornings like this one will cost you, or what they will give you instead.

Somewhere behind the sanctum a drum begins, slow and patient, as if it has all the time in the world to teach you how to listen.

Your mother kisses your forehead once, quickly, before the priests lead you further in, and you understand this is the last morning you belong only to her.

The bronze doors close behind you with a sound like distant thunder, and the girl who walked in is already, quietly, becoming someone else.

## Chapter Two: Years of Bells

Your teacher is an old man named Guru Pillai, and the first thing he teaches you is not a single step but how to stand perfectly, painfully still.

For a full season you learn nothing but how your feet should strike the stone floor, heel and ball together, until the sound becomes as familiar as your own heartbeat.

He ties small brass bells to your ankles and tells you the temple will always know exactly how honest your feet have been.

Some mornings your legs shake so badly from practice that you cannot climb the temple steps without gripping the cold granite railing.

You learn the shapes your hands must make for a lotus, for a deer, for a wave, twenty-eight small worlds folded into your ten fingers.

On the hottest afternoons you practice anyway, sweat stinging your eyes, because Guru Pillai says the body forgets nothing it is allowed to skip.

Slowly the songs called Thevaram enter you too, hymns older than your grandmother, until you can feel the rhythm arriving before the drummer's hand even falls.

There is a girl beside you named Chellam who cannot stop laughing during practice, and somehow her laughter makes the long afternoons bearable.

Once, exhausted, you weep quietly behind a pillar, certain your feet will never learn what your teacher demands of them.

Guru Pillai finds you there, says nothing about the tears, and simply begins counting the rhythm again until your feet remember on their own.

Years pass this way, each one nearly the same as the last, and yet each one leaves you a little more certain of your own hands.

The day finally comes when Guru Pillai looks at you differently, and says only that it is time you danced before more eyes than his own.

## Chapter Three: The Street of Dancers

You live now on the long street the old king built beside the temple, a whole row of houses for women who dance for the gods.

Every morning before the sun clears the tower, you help wash and dress the bronze image of the dancing god in silk finer than anything you own.

The temple gives you rice, oil, and a portion of gold each season, enough that your family no longer worries about the coming rains.

At the edge of the temple courtyard, a quiet young stone carver named Aran spends his days coaxing dancers out of plain grey granite.

You are not permitted to marry outside this life you were given, and some nights that fact sits heavier on your chest than you expect.

Still, you find yourself pausing near his work more often than duty requires, and he always seems to need exactly one more moment before you leave.

He never says much, only shows you, without a word, the stone dancer he has been shaping for months, and somehow her hands look like yours.

The nagaswaram and the tavil drum sound six times a day now, and your body has learned every one of their calls by heart.

You dance at dawn, at midday, and again as the lamps are lit, until performance no longer feels like performance, only breathing.

A pilgrim once weeps openly watching you dance the story of the god who danced the world into being, and afterward you cannot explain why that moved you more than any praise.

Word begins to travel through the town that the temple has, among its four hundred dancers, one whose hands the priests themselves stop to watch.

Aran hears this too, and says only, quietly, that he always knew, which is somehow the compliment you remember longest.

You tell yourself this street, these lamps, this drum, are enough of a life, and most days, truthfully, they nearly are.

But one evening a royal messenger arrives at the temple gate, and the whole street of dancers goes suddenly, unnaturally quiet.

## Chapter Four: The Night of the Standing King

The emperor himself is coming to Thanjavur to give thanks at his great temple, and the high priest has chosen you to dance before him.

Your hands go cold at the news, and for one whole night you cannot remember a single step Guru Pillai ever taught you.

Guru Pillai finds you at dawn and reminds you, firmly, that your feet have never once forgotten anything you gave them time to learn.

On the night itself, a thousand oil lamps line the courtyard, and the great tower seems to lean down out of the darkness to watch you too.

You are dressed in silk so fine it whispers when you move, your gold anklets heavier than any you have worn in practice.

The king, Rajaraja, sits beneath a canopy with his sister beside him, and for one terrifying breath you cannot make your feet begin.

Then the drum strikes, and your body simply remembers, the way it always has, before your mind even catches up to it.

You dance the story of the god of fire and destruction, one foot lifted in eternal motion, and the courtyard seems to hold its breath with you.

Midway through, your ankle bells ring out of rhythm for half a heartbeat, and only you and Guru Pillai, watching from the shadows, ever notice.

You correct it so smoothly that no one else in the courtyard seems to have felt anything but awe.

When the final pose settles and the drum falls silent, the silence afterward is louder than any applause could ever be.

Then the whole courtyard erupts, and you catch, just once, Aran's face among the crowd, lit gold by a thousand small flames.

The king himself sends word the next morning that your family's grant of land is to be doubled, though what you remember is only how the stone felt cool beneath your bare, aching feet.

## Chapter Five: What the Bronze Remembers

Years slide past the way the river does, steady and only noticed when you stop to look at how far it has carried you.

Chellam marries a merchant from the port and leaves the street of dancers, though she visits every festival with more children each time.

Aran finishes, at last, the stone dancer he began so long ago, and quietly asks the temple to set her in a corner where the morning light always finds her first.

You never marry as other women do, but you are not, in the way that matters to you, ever truly alone.

Guru Pillai grows too frail to demonstrate the steps himself, so instead he sits and simply hums the rhythm while you dance for him one more time.

The morning he dies, you dance anyway, because he always said the temple does not stop for anyone's grief, only slows enough to hold it.

Now a new girl stands ankle deep in the temple tank before dawn, and her mother combs river mud from her hair with trembling fingers.

You recognize the fear in her eyes, the same fear you once carried, and you kneel to tie the first bells onto her small ankles yourself.

You teach her, as you were taught, to stand still before you ever teach her to move, and to let her feet learn honesty first.

Some evenings you still visit the stone dancer in her corner of the courtyard, and trace the shape of a hand that has outlasted your own.

The bronze god still dances his eternal dance above the sanctum, one foot always lifted, never quite landing, exactly as he has for a hundred years.

And when your own knees finally ache too much to rise before dawn, you will still hear, somewhere close, small bells ringing in perfect, patient time.
