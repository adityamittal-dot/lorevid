# Research — Your Life as a Temple Dancer in the Chola Empire (RESERVE slot b)

Sources used (5+ distinct sites, all opened this session): prepp.in, hinduismtoday.com, cmi.ac.in,
worldhistory.org, academia.edu, rooftopapp.com, vajiramandravi.com, padhai.ai, dailyartmagazine.com.

1. The Brihadisvara (Brihadeeswarar) Temple at Thanjavur was built by Emperor Rajaraja I, reigned c. 985–1014 CE, and its inscriptions record that the temple maintained 400 dancing women (devadasis) along with their gurus and musicians — https://prepp.in/news/e-492-society-and-economy-of-chola-dynasty-medieval-india-history-notes
2. Rajaraja I housed the temple's 400 devadasis in two long streets he built specifically for them beside the temple, showing the scale of royal investment in the dance service — https://www.hinduismtoday.com/hpi/2025/01/21/temples-treasures-and-trade-the-astonishing-legacy-of-indias-chola-dynasty/
3. Temple inscriptions record daily disbursements to the dancers of oil, turmeric, betel leaves and areca nuts as part of their upkeep — https://prepp.in/news/e-492-society-and-economy-of-chola-dynasty-medieval-india-history-notes
4. Dancers received grants of land and fixed salaries from the temple in exchange for their service, making the devadasi role an economically secure, respected position rather than casual entertainment — https://www.academia.edu/82074668/Rajarajesvara_temple_inscriptions_A_patronage_to_dance_by_rajaraja_i
5. Dance and music were transmitted hereditarily within the Isai Vellalar community; women trained as devadasis while their male relatives trained as nattuvanars, the conductors who kept rhythm and supervised training — https://rooftopapp.com/art-wiki/bharatanatyam
6. A dancer's public debut, the arangetram, was held on an astrologically chosen auspicious day and could be performed in the presence of the king himself — https://rooftopapp.com/art-wiki/bharatanatyam
7. Some Chola-era temples performed six fixed daily rituals: Ushathkalam near dawn (about 5:30 a.m.), Kalasanthi (8:00 a.m.), Uchikalam (10:00 a.m.), Sayarakshai (5:00 p.m.), Irandamkalam (7:00 p.m.) and Ardha Jamam (8:00 p.m.), each including a sacred bath, decoration, food offering and the waving of lamps for the deity — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apathsahayar_Temple,_Thirupazhanam (cross-checked against temple-ritual pattern in prepp.in)
8. Temple worship was accompanied by the nagaswaram (a long reed pipe) and the tavil (a barrel drum), with priests chanting Vedic verses as worshippers prostrated before the temple's flag-mast — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apathsahayar_Temple,_Thirupazhanam
9. The Brihadisvara Temple's great tower (vimana) rises more than 200 feet (about 60 meters), among the tallest temple towers in India, built entirely of granite and completed in about seven years (1003–1010 CE) — https://prepp.in/news/e-492-society-and-economy-of-chola-dynasty-medieval-india-history-notes
10. The Chola state minted gold coins called pon, kasu and kalanju for large transactions, alongside silver coins; the copper kasu coin of this era carried a standing king on one face and a seated king on the other, a type begun under Rajaraja I — https://southindiancoins.blogspot.com/2009/10/chola-coins.html
11. Ordinary trade still ran largely on barter, with paddy (unhusked rice) itself used as a unit of exchange and even as a form of tax payment — https://prepp.in/news/e-492-society-and-economy-of-chola-dynasty-medieval-india-history-notes
12. The Kaveri river delta's irrigated paddy fields made rice the staple of the Chola heartland, producing surpluses large enough to fund temple building and long-distance trade — https://prepp.in/news/e-492-society-and-economy-of-chola-dynasty-medieval-india-history-notes
13. A single great temple could receive as much as 5,000 tons of rice a year in tribute and offerings drawn from conquered and allied territory across the south — https://vocal.media/history/the-wealth-of-the-chola-dynasty-power-prosperity-and-a-lasting-legacy
14. Temples under the Cholas functioned as banks and landholding institutions as well as shrines, lending grain and gold and financing irrigation tanks for the surrounding villages — https://prepp.in/news/e-492-society-and-economy-of-chola-dynasty-medieval-india-history-notes
15. Village administration ran through local self-governing assemblies (sabhas and urs) that managed irrigation, land records and local disputes with real autonomy from the distant royal court — https://prepp.in/news/e-492-society-and-economy-of-chola-dynasty-medieval-india-history-notes
16. Brahmins held special legal and economic privileges under the Cholas, including exemption from certain taxes, in return for their ritual and administrative duties inside the temple — https://prepp.in/news/e-492-society-and-economy-of-chola-dynasty-medieval-india-history-notes
17. Chola bronze icons — most famously Shiva as Nataraja, Lord of the Dance, one foot raised in motion, the other trampling the demon of ignorance, ringed in a circle of fire — were dressed daily in real silk garments, garlands and jewelry as if they were living beings before being carried out on palanquins amid drums and chanting (debated: some scholars read the fire-ring purely as cosmological symbol rather than direct dance-reference) — https://www.worldhistory.org/article/831/shiva-nataraja---lord-of-the-dance/
18. Both men and women in this period wore anklets; women's hollow gold anklets, called silambu, held loose pearls or pellets inside so that each step produced a distinct ringing or rattling sound — used deliberately by dancers to mark rhythm — https://www.cmi.ac.in/gift/Archeaology/arch_jewellery.htm
19. Rajaraja I's elder sister, the princess Kundavai, was a powerful patron of temple building and the arts in her own right and spent much of her later life at Thanjavur alongside her brother, giving Chola royal women real influence over temple culture (debated: her exact role in specific dance patronage decisions is not directly attested in inscriptions) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kundavai (cross-referenced in academia.edu inscriptions paper)
20. The Cholas built a navy powerful enough that the Bay of Bengal was effectively a "Chola lake," and Rajendra I's fleet campaigned as far as Srivijaya in maritime Southeast Asia, bringing Chinese, Arab and Southeast Asian goods and envoys through southern ports — https://www.accessiblelearning.in/the-chola-empire-how-a-tamil-dynasty-built-south-indias-greatest-maritime-superpower
21. The Chola dynasty rose to prominence under Vijayalaya (c. 841–878 CE), who took Thanjavur and built a temple to the goddess Nisumbhasudani there, making the city a Chola stronghold generations before Rajaraja's great temple — https://padhai.ai/history/medieval/chola-dynasty
22. The empire reached its territorial height under Rajaraja I and his son Rajendra I (r. 1014–1044), who together controlled the Pandya and Chera lands, northern Sri Lanka, Lakshadweep and the Maldives — https://padhai.ai/history/medieval/chola-dynasty
23. Silk and fine cotton textiles, dyed and traded through southern port towns, were markers of wealth and rank; temple deities and their attendant dancers were dressed in silk for major rituals while everyday wear for most people used plainer cotton — https://www.dailyartmagazine.com/chola-bronzes-devotion-in-metal/
24. Vedic instruction and temple learning were conducted largely in Tamil and Sanskrit, and inscriptions of the period were carved directly into temple stone in Tamil, preserving administrative and cultural detail (including dancers' names and duties) for a thousand years — https://prepp.in/news/e-492-society-and-economy-of-chola-dynasty-medieval-india-history-notes
25. The Chola Empire began a long decline after the mid-1200s as the Pandya and Hoysala kingdoms rose and succession disputes and costly wars drained the treasury; the last significant Chola ruler was defeated by the Pandyas around 1279 CE, though the royal line continued in name into the 14th century — https://padhai.ai/history/medieval/chola-dynasty

26. The Thevaram is the body of Tamil Shaiva devotional hymns composed by the poet-saints Appar, Sambandar and Sundarar in the seventh and eighth centuries CE, and by the Chola period these hymns were sung daily as part of temple worship in southern shrines — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thalai_Sanga_Nanmathiyam (cross-referenced against the Thevaram/Paadal Petra Sthalam pattern found across Chola-era temple pages)
