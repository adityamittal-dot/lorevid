# Research — Your Life as a Byzantine Silk Weaver (2026-09-26, RESERVE slot a)

Sources used (8 distinct domains): en.wikipedia.org, worldhistory.org, pallasweb.com, ebsco.com,
ancientwarhistory.com, ancient-origins.net, talesoftimesforgotten.com, history.com, smarthistory.org.

1. Constantinople's silk trade was organized into five specialized guilds regulated by the Book of the Eparch, issued under Emperor Leo VI around 911-912: raw-silk dealers (metaxoprates), silk-cloth vendors (bestioprates), Syrian silk sellers (prandioprates), silk-thread producers (katartarii) and weavers (serikarii). — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_the_Prefect
2. Certain fine grades of silk were forbidden from sale to foreigners outright, and any transaction with a foreign buyer had to be reported to the city's prefect, the Eparch. — https://ancientwarhistory.com/trade-and-control-in-10th-century-constantinople-the-byzantine-economic-system-under-the-book-of-the-eparch/
3. Guild weavers had to sell only through guild-approved merchant channels; hoarding silk or reselling it outside those channels carried penalties. — https://www.pallasweb.com/deesis/byzantine-regulation-of-the-silk-industry.html
4. A craftsman caught using scales or weights without the eparch's official seal could be publicly flogged and shaved. — https://www.pallasweb.com/deesis/byzantine-regulation-of-the-silk-industry.html
5. Byzantine silk workshops typically held around ten people and were often family operations spanning several generations under one roof. — https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1220/daily-life-in-the-byzantine-empire/
6. Weaving was one of the few trades fully open to women, though registered weavers were more often men. — https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1220/daily-life-in-the-byzantine-empire/
7. Girls in Byzantine households were taught spinning and weaving alongside scripture and the lives of the saints. — https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1212/women-in-the-byzantine-empire/
8. Aristocratic women could own or personally supervise a workshop, sometimes working from a household's segregated women's quarter, the gynaikonitis. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gynaeceum
9. Constantinople's own silk industry began after monks, on a mission for Emperor Justinian I in the mid-500s CE, smuggled silkworm eggs (or newly hatched larvae) out of China hidden inside hollow bamboo canes packed with straw. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smuggling_of_silkworm_eggs_into_the_Roman_Empire
10. Before that theft, Byzantium had to buy silk through Persian middlemen at prices contemporaries considered extortionate, which was part of Justinian's motive for wanting an independent supply. — https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/silk-worms-are-smuggled-byzantine-empire/
11. Justinian's law reserved true imperial "Tyrian" purple, made from thousands of crushed murex sea snails, for the emperor alone; unauthorized use was treated as usurping imperial insignia. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrian_purple
12. A single ounce of genuine Tyrian purple dye could be worth more than its weight in gold, since producing it required thousands of snails. — https://www.ancient-origins.net/weird-facts/roman-purple-dye-0019607
13. By tradition only a child born to a reigning emperor inside the purple-draped birthing chamber of the palace could be called porphyrogenitus, "born in the purple." — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_in_the_purple
14. The main Byzantine coin was the gold nomisma (solidus), about 4.4 grams and roughly 95.8 percent pure gold, minted mainly at Constantinople. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solidus_(coin)
15. One nomisma could pay an ordinary laborer for roughly 20-25 days of work; a sixth-century construction worker earning nine bronze folles a day needed about 32 days' wages to equal one nomisma. — https://talesoftimesforgotten.com/2020/03/18/how-much-were-gold-coins-really-worth-during-the-middle-ages/
16. A loaf of bread in Constantinople could cost as little as one to three bronze coins in ordinary times, though wheat prices spiked sharply during shortages. — https://talesoftimesforgotten.com/2020/03/18/how-much-were-gold-coins-really-worth-during-the-middle-ages/
17. Wealthier residents lived in two-story brick houses built around an inner courtyard; poorer residents crowded into multi-story wood or stone tenements with no glass in the windows. — https://www.pallasweb.com/deesis/daily-life-in-constantinople.html
18. Many houses had their own water cisterns, and the city held thousands of public and private cisterns; where there was none, water was delivered door to door on carts. — https://www.pallasweb.com/deesis/daily-life-in-constantinople.html
19. Bread was baked in shared communal dome ovens, supplied with firewood delivered by commercial fuel merchants. — https://www.pallasweb.com/deesis/daily-life-in-constantinople.html
20. Markets mixed local produce with silk from China, spices from India and grain from Egypt, all sold under officially stamped weights and measures that state inspectors checked to stop profiteering. — https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1220/daily-life-in-the-byzantine-empire/
21. Ships unloaded raw silk, spices and grain at warehouses lining the Golden Horn, the city's sheltered natural harbor, which could be closed off in danger by a great chain stretched across its mouth. — https://istanbultarihi.ist/565-seaport-of-the-empire-istanbul-and-shipping-trade-network
22. Before large bells became common, Byzantine churches and monasteries summoned worshippers by beating a long wooden plank called a semantron with a mallet, a practice traced back to at least the sixth century. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantron
23. The Plague of Justinian struck Constantinople from 541-542 CE; the contemporary historian Procopius claimed a peak of ten thousand deaths a day in the capital, though modern historians think the true daily figure was likely lower (debated). — https://www.worldhistory.org/article/782/justinians-plague-541-542-ce/
24. Modern historians estimate the plague killed roughly a fifth to two-fifths of Constantinople's population before it eased (debated exact toll). — https://www.history.com/articles/plague-justinian-pandemic
25. In January 532, the chariot-racing factions known as the Blues and Greens united in the Hippodrome chanting "Nika" ("Win!") and rioted for days, burning nearly half the city, including the original Hagia Sophia church. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nika_riots
26. Justinian's general Belisarius put down the Nika riot by trapping the rioters inside the Hippodrome; ancient sources claim as many as thirty thousand were killed there (debated figure). — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nika_riots
27. Justinian rebuilt an even grander Hagia Sophia and dedicated it in 537 CE; its earliest interior decoration was plain crosses and geometric patterns rather than pictures of saints. — https://smarthistory.org/middle-byzantine-mosaics-hagia-sophia/
28. Figural mosaics of Christ, the Virgin Mary and saints were mostly added to Hagia Sophia in later centuries, and were themselves removed or covered for a time during the Iconoclastic Controversy of the eighth and ninth centuries, when religious images were banned (debated how completely the ban was enforced). — https://smarthistory.org/middle-byzantine-mosaics-hagia-sophia/
