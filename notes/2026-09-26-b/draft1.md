# Draft 1 — Your Life as an Ottoman Janissary

narrator_voice: am_onyx | ambience: fire | music_mood: epic_soft

## Chapter 1: The Scouts Come to the Village

By supper you will not be your mother's son anymore, and no one in the village has told you yet. (tense)
Riders came at dawn, official riders, with a decree that every household must bring its sons before them. (calm)
You are eight, barefoot in the mud of a Balkan mountain village, watching the scout study your face too long. (calm)
He wants strong boys, sharp boys, boys whose eyes do not drop when a stranger looks straight into them. (calm)
Your mother grips your shoulder so hard it will leave a bruise, though her face stays carefully, terribly still. (sad)
Fathers have been told this comes every few years, whenever the sultan's household needs new blood to raise. (calm)
You do not understand yet that you are chosen, only that the scout has stopped walking past your door. (tense)
By evening your name is on a list, and your mother is folding your one good shirt with shaking hands. (sad)
She presses a small carved comb into your palm and tells you to remember the sound of this river. (tender)
At the village edge, a line of boys is already forming, none of them older than you are. (calm)
None of you cry where the riders can see it, though the youngest keeps his fists closed very tight. (sad)
The road north swallows the mountain behind you, and somewhere past it, a new life is already waiting. (reflective)

## Chapter 2: Learning a New Tongue

For three years you belong to a farming family in the hills of Anatolia, and slowly, you belong to them. (calm)
They teach you Turkish over shared bread, and the shape of prayers your own mother would not have known. (calm)
You learn to drive oxen, mend fences, and hold your tongue when homesickness rises hot behind your eyes. (calm)
At night the farmer's wife hums a lullaby close enough to your own mother's that you have to look away. (tender)
Officers ride out once to watch you work, silent, marking something in a ledger you are never shown. (tense)
One spring morning you are called to Istanbul, and the farm family sends you off with dried figs and blessings. (warm)
The barracks at Aksaray rise grey and enormous ahead of you, nothing like any building your village could have imagined. (awe)
You are Acemi now, a cadet, one of hundreds learning to read, to pray, and to hold a blade steady. (calm)
Officers watch which boys are quickest with numbers and which are quickest with a bow, and they write it all down. (calm)
The sharpest, tallest boys are quietly sent instead to the palace school inside the walls of Topkapı itself. (awe)
You are not chosen for the palace, and some nights that stings more than the drilling ever does. (sad)
But your arms grow strong, your Turkish grows easy, and something in you starts to answer to a new name. (reflective)

## Chapter 3: The Brotherhood of the Spoon

The day you join a full company, an officer calls you by a title stranger than any you have worn. (calm)
Your captain is named for soup, çorbacı, and beneath him serve a head cook and a head water carrier. (calm)
The whole corps is called the Ocak, the hearth, and your company's hearth is one great iron cauldron. (warm)
You eat from that cauldron every day now, and you are told plainly what the meal really means. (calm)
To take food from the sultan's kazan is to swear yourself to him, one spoon at a time. (awe)
Men who eat, drill, and sleep beside that cauldron for years start calling each other brothers of the spoon. (warm)
You are given a tall white felt cap, a börk, its flap said to echo a wandering holy man's sleeve. (calm)
His name was Hacı Bektaş Veli, and his order becomes yours, its dervishes standing in as your chaplains. (calm)
Discipline here is never gentle, and a foot beaten raw in front of the company teaches lessons no lecture ever could. (tense)
You are forbidden marriage, forbidden any outside trade, told the Ocak alone is the only family a Janissary ever needs. (sad)
Every quarter you are paid in silver akçe, with more set aside for your clothing and your blade. (calm)
Once a year, on the fifteenth night of Ramadan, the sultan himself sends trays of baklava to your barracks. (warm)
Two men to a pole, you carry the sweet trays home singing, the only softness this life allows you. (tender)
You learn later what it means when a company overturns its own cauldron in the yard at night. (tense)
It means mutiny, and every man here understands that losing a cauldron to an enemy is the deepest shame. (tense)
By your third winter in the Ocak, you no longer remember missing anyone, only that you once did. (reflective)

## Chapter 4: The Walls of Constantinople

Spring finds your whole company marching toward walls older than any building you have ever stood beside in your life. (calm)
Mehmed, your young sultan, has brought cannon so large that four hundred men are needed to haul just one. (awe)
For fifty days the guns batter Constantinople's walls while you and your company wait behind the siege lines. (tense)
At night the mehter band strikes up, drums and reed pipes so loud they seem to shake the ground itself. (urgent)
Officers say the music is meant to frighten the defenders and steady the nerves of men like you. (calm)
Before dawn on the final assault you check your matchlock gun by touch, since your hands already know it blind. (tense)
Your company carries its cauldron close behind the lines, and no one speaks of what losing it would mean. (tense)
The order comes, and the noise that follows is bigger than any sound your village ever taught you to expect. (urgent)
You do not remember every hour of that morning, only smoke, and shouting, and the weight of your own gun. (tense)
By afternoon the ancient walls have fallen, and the city that stood a thousand years belongs to your sultan now. (awe)
You are twenty eight years old, and you understand, quietly, that history has just walked directly through you. (reflective)
That night your captain lets the cauldron fires burn late, and no one orders anyone to stop singing. (warm)

## Chapter 5: An Old Soldier's Istanbul

Years pass gently after that, campaign by campaign, until your knees ache before the winter rains even arrive. (calm)
You are made an instructor now, teaching frightened new cadets the very same drills you once feared yourself. (warm)
You still eat from a cauldron every day, though your hands have grown too stiff for the fastest drills. (calm)
Young Acemi boys ask if you were truly at Constantinople, and you tell it slower each year you answer. (reflective)
You still keep the small carved comb your mother pressed into your hand on the road out of the mountains. (tender)
Some nights the mehter drums still echo somewhere in the barracks yard, and your body remembers before your mind does. (reflective)
You never married, never kept a house of your own, exactly as this life once promised you it would be. (sad)
But hundreds of brothers have shared your cauldron over the years, and not one of them was ever truly a stranger. (warm)
On the fifteenth night of Ramadan you still help carry the sweet trays, though younger arms do the lifting now. (tender)
The white felt cap still sits by your bed, older now, softened, shaped permanently to the memory of your own head. (calm)
You think, some evenings, of a river in a mountain village whose sound you can still almost hear. (reflective)
You were taken from one family as a frightened boy and quietly given another, spoon by spoon, year by year. (warm)
Tonight the fire in the hearth burns low, familiar, warm, and you let yourself finally, fully, completely rest. (calm)
