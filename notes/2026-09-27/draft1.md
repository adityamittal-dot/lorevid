# Your Life as an Egyptian Pyramid Builder — Draft 1

## Chapter One: The Flood Calls You Away

The river rises past your ankle, then your knee, and by morning your barley field is a lake you cannot walk.
A man from the district office reads your name from a list, and just like that, your season belongs to the king.
You have three months before the water falls again, he says, and three months to feed your family with someone else's grain.
Your wife packs bread, a jar of beer, a spare loincloth, and does not let you see her face while she does it.
This labor is owed, not chosen, the way grain tax is owed, and every man on your street has paid it before with his own hands.
The road south is already full of other men from other flooded fields, all walking the same direction, all carrying the same list.
Someone ahead of you is singing, low and rough, and by the second mile everyone nearby has joined the same three notes.
You cross a canal on a raft poled by a boy younger than your own son, and he does not charge you anything.
At the horizon, something pale and unfinished breaks the flat brown land, taller than any building you have ever stood beneath.
It does not look like stone from this distance. It looks like a second sunrise that has forgotten how to set.
By the time you reach the checkpoint, the sun has burned your neck raw, and a scribe waves you through without looking up from his list.

## Chapter Two: The City of the Living

The camp is not a camp. It is streets, real streets, lined with mud-brick houses that already have families living in them.
Smoke rises from bakeries on every corner, and underneath the bread smell is something sweeter, warmer — beer, brewing in vats as tall as you.
An overseer with ink-stained fingers assigns you to a crew, and tells you its name is the Vigorous Gang, and to be worthy of it.
Your crewmates are farmers too, from villages you have never heard of, and by the second bowl of stew you already know their children's names.
The Vigorous Gang is split into smaller bands with their own marks, so that even inside one crew, a man still belongs to something smaller and closer.
At dusk, farmers drive in cattle and sheep for the evening meal, and you are handed a cut of meat better than any feast day at home.
Someone explains the ten-day week to you: nine days of work, one day that belongs to your own aching legs and nothing else.
You sleep in a long gallery with dozens of other men, and the sound of that many people breathing becomes, within a week, comforting.
At night the village hums with small fires, someone's flute, someone else arguing gently about whose turn it is to fetch water.
Your beer ration arrives in a clay jar each evening, thick and sour-sweet, and you understand now why men call it their second wage.
Nobody here calls you a slave. The scribe who counts you each morning calls you by your name, and writes it down as if it matters.

## Chapter Three: The Weight You Carry

Your first task is nothing grand. You wet sand ahead of a loaded sledge, pouring water from a jar, one careful stripe at a time.
The stone groans forward easier than you expected, twenty men pulling where forty would have strained, and someone laughs at how simple the trick is.
Too much water and the sand turns to mud that swallows the sledge whole, so the trick is a careful hand, not a heavy one.
Later you are the twenty. Ropes bite your shoulder, your bare feet find the wet track, and the block moves only because everyone pulls on the same breath.
By midday the sun has weight of its own, pressing down on the back of your neck like a second load you never agreed to carry.
You think of boats you saw at the quarry landing, wide-bellied vessels bringing pale limestone up the flooded river, faster than any cart could manage.
A man named Merer keeps a written count of every trip his boats make, and you wonder what it feels like to be trusted with numbers.
The air over the causeway smells of dust and river mud and, faintly, of the bread ovens working somewhere behind you.
On your fourth week, a block shifts wrong and crushes two fingers of the man beside you, and the whole crew goes silent at once.
He is carried, not left, to healers who set the bones straight with wooden splints, and you are told plainly: this is why you are fed so well.
That night you cannot sleep for thinking of his face, pale and steady, refusing to make a sound while the splint was tied.
In the morning he is propped against the gallery wall, grinning, already telling the story wrong on purpose to make the younger men laugh.

## Chapter Four: Your Name in the Stone

Months in, your hands have stopped blistering and started simply being hard, the way a field path packs hard under enough footsteps.
The pyramid no longer looks unfinished from a distance. It looks like something climbing, one pale course at a time, toward whatever waits at the top.
Your foreman lets your crew carve its name into a stone destined for a hidden chamber, somewhere no living eye will ever see it again.
You watch a man scratch "Friends of Khufu" into soft rock with a chisel point, and you add your own crew's mark beside his, unhurried.
It will be sealed inside forever, he tells you, and somehow that is the part that makes your chest go tight rather than the height of the thing.
Nobody who buys bread from you at home will ever know this stone exists, and still you find yourself wanting it to be perfect anyway.
You trace the chisel marks with your thumb before the block is hauled away forever, as if you could keep the shape of it in your skin.
That evening, a storyteller in the gallery says the pharaoh's soul will climb these very stones toward the sun god when his time comes.
You do not fully believe it, or fully disbelieve it either, but you like imagining your own hands somewhere inside that long, patient climb.
The season's hardest week comes near the end, hauling the heaviest blocks yet, when even the strongest men in your crew go quiet with effort.
You finish that week with torn palms and a strange, aching pride you did not expect to feel over a pile of stacked limestone.

## Chapter Five: What Outlasts You

Word comes that the flood outside has finally dropped, the fields drying to the color of old bread, ready for seed again.
Your name is called from the same list that brought you here, and this time it releases you rather than claims you.
You say goodbye to the Vigorous Gang the way men say goodbye who expect, without saying so, to be called up again next flood.
The scribe crosses your name off his list the same way he wrote it on, without ceremony, though it feels like ceremony to you.
The man with the healed fingers walks you partway down the road, still favoring one hand, still laughing louder than anyone else in the gang.
You look back once at the pyramid, smaller now behind you, and it no longer looks like a second sunrise. It looks like something you helped make.
Walking home, you pass fields already softening, the black silt left by the river waiting for your plow the way it waits every year.
Your wife sees you from the doorway before you reach it, and does not ask why your palms are scarred, only how hungry you are.
That night you sleep in your own bed for the first time in months, and somehow it feels stranger than the crowded gallery ever did.
Years later, an old man now, you will tell your grandchildren about the stone with your crew's name pressed into it, hidden and safe.
You will not describe the height, or the sun, or the ache in your arms. You will simply say: I helped build something that was built to last.
Somewhere under all that pale stone, your name still waits in the dark, patient, unseen, and entirely yours.
