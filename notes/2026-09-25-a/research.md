# Research — Your Life as an Aztec Merchant in Tenochtitlan

1. Pochteca were a specialized, hereditary class of professional long-distance traders in the Aztec Empire, based above all in Tenochtitlan-Tlatelolco and active roughly from the fourteenth to the early sixteenth century — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pochteca
2. Pochteca ranked below the noble class but above the ordinary commoner (macehualli); their wealth and their military-intelligence role gave them an unusually high status for people who were not born nobles — https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/social-sciences-and-humanities/pochteca
3. A pochteca apprenticeship began around age ten inside the family and guild network, with boys starting on short local routes before graduating to full long-distance ventures — https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/social-sciences-and-humanities/pochteca
4. A boy who survived his first long-distance trading expedition earned the title tlazcaltiltin, marking his passage from apprentice to trading man — https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/social-sciences-and-humanities/pochteca
5. Because the Aztec world had no wheeled vehicles and no domesticated pack animals, all trade goods were moved by human porters, tlamemeh, who were trained from childhood to carry loads of about thirty-five kilograms on their backs for up to fifteen hours of walking — https://www.worldhistory.org/article/845/aztec-society/
6. Membership in a pochteca calpulli (guild ward) was hereditary and selective; a new member needed both the guild's consent and the approval of the calpulli's lord — https://www.vaia.com/en-us/explanations/archaeology/arcaheology-of-trade/aztec-trade-systems/
7. Pochteca traded in prestige goods carried across the empire and beyond it: quetzal and other tropical bird feathers, jaguar pelts, gold dust, turquoise, jade, seashells, cacao beans, and fine cotton cloaks — https://britannica.com/topic/pochteca
8. To avoid being robbed by their own countrymen on the road out of the capital, pochteca customarily departed from and returned to Tenochtitlan at night, and dressed in plain, humble clothing to hide how wealthy they actually were — https://mexicounexplained.com/pochteca-aztec-traders-aztec-spies/
9. Beyond trading, pochteca served the emperor as informants and spies; one specialized branch, the naualoztomeca ("disguised traders"), learned the language and customs of a foreign territory to gather intelligence, while another, the quimichi ("mice"), worked undercover inside rival city-states, often moving and observing city defenses at night — https://www.chipchick.com/2024/10/long-distance-traders-known-as-the-pochteca-also-served-as-the-aztec-empires-spies-gathering-intel-on-communities-in-other-lands
10. Before a major trading expedition, pochteca held ceremonies to their patron god Yacatecuhtli, tying their walking canes into a bundle each night and sprinkling them with blood drawn from their own ears, believing this secured safe travel and protection from robbers and wild animals — https://www.aztecopw.com/yacatecuhtli.html
11. Because pochteca faced such constant danger on the road, a trader who died away from home was given the funeral honors of a warrior: cremation with the tools of his trade, jaguar skins, quetzal feathers and fine jewelry to accompany him, and burial in an elevated place associated with the sun — https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/social-sciences-and-humanities/pochteca
12. Tenochtitlan was founded, according to Mexica tradition, in the year 1325 CE on a small island in Lake Texcoco, after the god Huitzilopochtli's sign of an eagle perched on a prickly-pear cactus devouring a snake was seen exactly as prophesied — https://www.britannica.com/topic/Huitzilopochtli
13. The city was linked to the mainland by three great causeways — north to Tepeyacac, south to Iztapalapa, and west to Tlacopan — and was crossed internally by canals so that canoes, not carts, were the normal way to move people and goods — https://www.britannica.com/topic/pre-Columbian-civilizations/Tenochtitlan
14. Much of the city's food was grown on chinampas, long narrow "floating gardens" built up in the shallow lake bed and separated by canals just wide enough for a canoe to pass — https://www.ancient-origins.net/ancient-places-americas/chinampas-001537
15. The great market at nearby Tlatelolco drew a reported sixty thousand buyers and sellers on its busiest trading days, astonishing the Spanish conquistador Bernal Diaz del Castillo, whose companions who had seen Rome and Constantinople said they had never seen a market so large or so orderly — https://www.mexicolore.co.uk/aztecs/aztefacts/great-market-tlatelolco
16. There was no coined money: cacao beans, plain cotton cloaks, and hollow bird quills filled with gold dust served as currency, and Hernan Cortes recorded that a single copper hatchet could be traded for eight thousand cacao beans — https://jayarrchocolate.com/blog/aztec-cacao-currency-history/
17. Every kind of merchandise at Tlatelolco had its own fixed row and place, from slaves and cloth to food and small goods, all priced in cacao beans, and market officials called pochtecatlailotlac patrolled constantly, checking quality and destroying false measures — https://www.mexicolore.co.uk/aztecs/aztefacts/great-market-tlatelolco
18. Aztec market inspectors used graded wooden boxes such as the quauhchiquihuitl, divided into fractions as small as a twelfth, to measure dry goods like maize, and graded leather bags, cups and jars to measure liquids and cacao beans, with a standing court of judges at the market center to punish counterfeiters and cheats on the spot — https://nist.medium.com/before-the-melting-pot-pre-columbian-weights-and-measures-5eec8ff9a042
19. Sumptuary laws set by the ruler Moctezuma I and recorded in the Codex Mendoza restricted cotton cloth, dyed and feathered cloaks, and gold or quetzal-feather headdresses to nobles and honored warriors; commoners were required to wear plain garments woven from maguey-cactus fiber, and the penalty for a commoner caught wearing cotton could be death (debated: the exact severity and consistency of enforcement across the empire is disputed among historians) — https://www.historyskills.com/classroom/year-8/aztec-clothes/
20. The tilma, a simple square or rectangular cloak knotted at one shoulder, was the standard outer garment for Aztec men of every class; its length, cloth and decoration were what marked a wearer's rank, from a plain knee-length maguey cloak on a commoner to an ankle-length, feather-worked cloak on a noble or a warrior who had taken captives in battle — https://www.mexicolore.co.uk/aztecs/artefacts/tilmatli-cape-or-cloak
21. The ordinary Aztec diet centered on maize, prepared as tortillas, tamales, or a thin gruel called atole, supplemented with beans, squash, chile peppers and amaranth; meat was a rare addition, since the only domesticated food animals were turkeys, ducks and a hairless breed of dog — https://www.worldhistory.org/article/723/aztec-food--agriculture/
22. Most Aztecs ate two meals a day, the first after some hours of morning work and the second in the hottest part of the afternoon around three o'clock; a common meal was only two or three tortillas with beans and chile sauce, and tortillas made fresh at dawn were also carried, cold, as travel food by merchants and soldiers on the road — https://www.worldhistory.org/article/723/aztec-food--agriculture/
23. Teaching a daughter to grind maize and pat out an even, round tortilla by hand was considered one of the essential lessons a mother owed her — https://www.worldhistory.org/article/723/aztec-food--agriculture/
24. Commoner households commonly kept a small garden plot, the calmil, attached to the home to grow supplementary food for the family — https://www.worldhistory.org/article/723/aztec-food--agriculture/
25. Pochteca doubled as the empire's forward intelligence service, and the killing of Mexica merchants operating abroad was treated as a legitimate cause for war: sources describe Ahuitzotl going to war against Oaxaca after Aztec traders and agents there were killed in the 1490s, and, separately, an alliance of Basin of Mexico city-states going to war to avenge a group of vanguard merchants slain in Anahuac during Moctezuma's reign (debated: exact dates, place names and casualty details vary between sources) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahuitzotl and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pochteca
26. Wealthy pochteca who returned successfully from a major expedition marked it with a private feast for their own trading guild rather than public display, in keeping with the custom of concealing their wealth from the wider city — https://theeyehuatulco.com/2024/06/29/the-pochteca/
27. Everyday commerce and merchant academic sources on the Tlatelolco marketplace are gathered and cross-checked in Cambridge University Press's scholarly survey "Everyday Life in the Aztec World," which devotes a full chapter to the merchant class and its customs — https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/everyday-life-in-the-aztec-world/merchant/FD80A5E407DFEB532219756016CA3269
