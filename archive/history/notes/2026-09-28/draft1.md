# Your Life as a Roman Centurion's Wife (Draft 1)

## Chapter 1: The Vicus by the Wall
By the time the evening horn sounds, you already know whether the cohort is marching home tonight, or whether you will light the lamp for no one again.
Two winters ago you were a farmer's daughter beyond the ditches; now you keep a hearth in the vicus, married in every way but the one Rome recognizes.
Your husband is called Rufus, a Batavian horseman posted to this fort on the wall's cold spine, and Rome still calls you nobody's wife at all.
The gate guard knows your face by now, waves you through the mud without asking, though your marriage exists only in the two of you, and no ledger.
Your house is one narrow room behind a leather-worker's stall, wattle walls, a hard-packed floor, and a hearth you have learned to coax from wet British wood.
You remember the first night the wind came off the moor and found every gap in the wattle, and Rufus laughed and called it a proper wedding gift.
He is gone eleven days out of every thirty on patrol or errand, and each departure you tell yourself the same lie, that this time will feel ordinary.
This morning he tied his cloak wrong twice, kissed your forehead, and said nothing of the rumor that the northern tribes are gathering beyond the last watchtower.
You did not ask him to promise anything, because a soldier's promise is only ever a kindness, and you have learned to want him home instead of words.
Rome will not write your name beside his, but the neighbor women already call you wife, and that, for now, is the only citizenship you actually need.

## Chapter 2: Bread, Beer and Barley
Morning begins with barley bread and a cup of thin, sour beer, since the fort's water tastes of iron and the beer, oddly, tastes of home.
You trade eggs for bacon-fat at the corner stall, haggling in a mixture of your mother's tongue and the soldiers' Latin, understood by everyone and owned by no one.
The vicus smells of woodsmoke, wet wool, and the leather-worker's tannin next door, a smell you once hated and now cannot imagine falling asleep without.
At the crossroads shrine you leave a bent coin for the household Lares each morning, asking only that they watch the road Rufus rides out on.
You have heard the soldiers pray, too, to a discipline-goddess whose altar stands near the gate, as if discipline alone could out-argue a British winter.
Once a decurion sent word to the commander's wife that the garrison's beer had run dry entirely, and the whole vicus laughed about it for a week.
You have learned to read weather in smoke, to salt fish before frost, to patch a roof before the rain finds the exact seam that failed last year.
In autumn a supply cart brings oysters, honey, and dried apples up from the south, and for one evening the whole street smells like somewhere warmer.
Your neighbor lost her own soldier last spring to fever, not battle, and still keeps his cloak folded on the shelf where he used to hang it.
You have learned not to flinch when she says his name, because grief here is common enough that flinching would be its own kind of rudeness.
In the evenings you mend socks by firelight, the same coarse wool socks the soldiers wear pulled over bare feet inside their sandals against the cold.
Rufus once brought you a comb carved from bone, traded for a season's wages, and you still do not know which of you was more delighted.

## Chapter 3: The Campaign Season
Then the horn sounds differently, three short blasts instead of one, and every woman in the vicus stops what she is doing at exactly the same moment.
The cohort is ordered north, past the last milecastle, to answer a raid that burned two farmsteads and left the road smelling of char and wet ash.
Rufus packs in the dark so as not to wake you, though you have been awake the whole time, watching the shape of him against the low fire.
He tells you, quietly, where he has hidden three months of pay under the hearthstone, in case the answer to this campaign is the one neither of you says aloud.
You watch the cohort file out past the gate at dawn, hooves loud on frozen mud, until the column is a dark line and then nothing at all.
The first week you keep busy on purpose, because idle hands invite the kind of thinking that does no one, least of all you, any good.
By the second week, every rider approaching the gate makes your whole body go still before your mind has decided whether to hope or brace.
A runner comes with a strength report for the commander, and the vicus reads his face like a letter before anyone dares ask him a single question.
The news, when it comes, is neither the best nor the worst: skirmishes, one cohort mauled badly, yours untouched so far, still somewhere beyond the last signal tower.
That night you sit at the lararium longer than usual, and for once you do not haggle with the household gods, you simply ask, plainly, for him.
A week later a wounded rider passes through needing a surgeon, and for one terrible breath before you see his face clearly, you believe it might be Rufus.
It is not him, and you are ashamed of the relief you feel, because somewhere beyond the wall another woman is about to feel the opposite.
You sit with the wounded man's own wife through the worst of the night, holding a lamp steady while the surgeon works, because that, too, is what waiting means here.
Spring arrives before any final word does, the first green pushing up through the mud around the vicus wells, indifferent to who is still missing.
Then, on an ordinary grey morning, a letter comes north with the supply train, sealed with wax and written in a hand you would know anywhere.

## Chapter 4: The Letter in Spring
The letter says only that he is well, that the cohort held the line at a river crossing, and that he will be home by the barley harvest.
You read it standing in the doorway, then sitting on the step, then a third time by the fire, as if the words might change on a fourth reading.
When the cohort finally rides back through the gate, dust-grey and thinner than they left, you do not run, you simply stand where he can find you.
Rufus finds you first, and for a long moment neither of you speaks, because some homecomings are too large for the vicus's ordinary Latin.
He has a new scar above one eyebrow and a story he tells badly and often, and you decide you will let him tell it badly for years.
That first night home he sleeps twelve hours straight, and you sit awake beside him simply to watch him breathe, which feels like the most useful thing you have ever done.
He brings back, of all things, a small bronze bell taken in trade from a trader beyond the wall, and hangs it by your door for luck.
Slowly the ordinary returns: barley bread, sour beer, the leather-worker's tannin smell, arguments about nothing, laughter about less, the small tyranny of daily chores.
You notice, without saying it aloud, that he counts his remaining years of service now the way other men count coin, carefully, and often.
He has eleven years left before his discharge, before the diploma that will finally make Rome call you what your neighbors already call you.
You do not need a bronze document to know what you are to each other, but you have learned exactly how much easier the world is for those Rome recognizes.
So you wait again, differently now, not for a horn or a rider, but for a number of years to quietly, patiently, run out.

## Chapter 5: The Bronze Diploma
The years move the way frontier years do, in seasons rather than days: campaigns, harvests, a daughter born loud in autumn, a son born quiet in spring.
Your daughter is nine before she understands why other children can say "citizen" and she, for now, cannot, and you tell her the truth plainly, without bitterness.
Rufus grows grey at the temples and steadier in the shoulders, less patrol, more training younger men who remind you both, painfully, of who he once was.
On the morning of his final formal parade, you stand at the edge of the parade ground with both children, and you are the one who cannot stop shaking.
The commander reads out the names of the discharged in a flat, official voice, until Rufus's own name lands in the cold air like something enormous.
Afterward a clerk hands him a folded sheet of bronze, small enough to hold in one palm, heavier than anything you have ever carried together.
It grants him citizenship, and it grants your children citizenship, and it grants you, at last, the plain legal word wife, twenty-five years late and exactly on time.
That evening the whole vicus comes to your door with beer and bread, because everyone here has waited out someone's twenty-five years at least once.
You pin the diploma's two bronze leaves above the lararium, beside the household gods who listened to twenty-five winters of the same quiet request.
Rufus, older now, still ties his cloak wrong sometimes, and you still fix it without being asked, the way you have for a quarter of a century.
You will grow old within sight of the wall you once watched him ride out from, the mud and woodsmoke turned, somehow, into the only home you know.
On the coldest nights you still hear that first wind finding the gaps in the wattle, and you still call it, quietly, a proper wedding gift.
Rome took twenty-five years to say your name, but the vicus said it the first winter, and in the end, you realize, that was the citizenship that mattered.
