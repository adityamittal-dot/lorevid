# Draft 1 — Your Life as a Maratha Cavalry Soldier

narrator_voice: bm_george (fits a soldier's life; bm_lewis and am_onyx used in the last two scripts)
ambience: wind
music_mood: epic_soft

## Chapter 1: The Recruiting Ground

[calm] Before sunrise you are only a farmer's son, standing barefoot on cracked earth, listening to a horse breathe in the dark.

[calm] The dry season has come, and with it a rider from the hill fort, calling every able young man to the maidan below Rajgad.

[warm] Your father presses a small brass token into your palm, the one he carried when he rode as a bargir twenty years before.

[calm] You have never owned a horse of your own, so today you will stand among the bargirs, given a mount and a sword from the state's own stock.

[warm] The recruiting officer studies your shoulders, your grip, the calluses already hard from field work, and nods once.

[tender] Your mother does not cry where you can see her; she only ties a thread of red cotton around your wrist and says nothing at all.

[calm] By afternoon you are one of forty new riders learning to sit a horse that does not yet trust you.

[calm] The Bhimthadi they give you is small and unlovely, bred from desert stock and hill ponies, built for distance rather than beauty.

[reflective] An old shiledar, a trooper who owns his own horse and sword, tells you that a Maratha rider survives on speed, not steel.

[warm] He shows you how to wrap the reins so your hand never slips, how to feel a slope through the horse's shoulders before you see it.

[calm] At dusk you eat bhakri baked on a flat stone, jowar bread dark and cracked, and it tastes better than anything from your mother's kitchen.

[reflective] But the letter that reaches the fort at nightfall changes everything: the Nizam's army is moving north, and Rajgad's riders are ordered to the Godavari within the week.

## Chapter 2: The Dry-Season March

[calm] You learn quickly that a campaign begins with waiting, then becomes nothing but motion.

[calm] The column rides before light, while the grass still holds the night's coolness, and rests through the worst heat of the day.

[warm] Each man carries roasted gram, a little rice, raw sugar wrapped in cloth, and flour to bake once the column finally halts.

[tense] Word passes down the line without a single trumpet: ride wide of the river villages, take nothing from the fields, harm no woman or child.

[calm] You have heard this rule since childhood, but only now do you understand its weight, riding past farmers who do not flinch as you pass.

[awe] Bahirji Naik's men move ahead of the column disguised as pilgrims and grain merchants, and it is said the Peshwa knows the enemy's numbers before the enemy has finished counting them.

[calm] At the evening halt you groom the Bhimthadi before you eat, because the old shiledar says a hungry horse kills its rider faster than any sword.

[tender] Around the cooking fires men speak of Bhavani, the goddess of the Bhosale clan, and you touch the red thread on your wrist and think of your mother's hands.

[reflective] Somewhere ahead, the Nizam's army marches slow and heavy, dragging cannon and a baggage train longer than your entire village.

[calm] Your captain says this slowness is the only weapon you need: what cannot run cannot escape.

[urgent] Then, on the ninth day, a rider comes back at a gallop, and the whole column turns without a single word of complaint.

## Chapter 3: Palkhed

[tense] By the time you reach the plain near Palkhed, the Nizam's great army has already been circled like a bullock pinned inside a fence of its own baggage carts.

[calm] Your wing is sent to the Godavari, and your captain says only one thing: nothing drinks from this river tonight but us.

[tense] You do not fight in ranks the way the old stories describe; you ride, wheel, and vanish before the enemy guns can turn toward you.

[urgent] A cannon fires once from the Nizam's line, and the shot tears past close enough that your horse screams and nearly throws you.

[calm] You steady him with your knees the way the old shiledar taught you, and somehow you are still mounted when the smoke clears.

[sad] By the river you find a boy no older than your younger brother, a Nizam's trooper, dead beside a mount that will not leave his body.

[reflective] You are ordered on before you can think too long about him, and that, more than the cannon, is the thing that stays with you.

[calm] For three days you and your wing hold every crossing and every well, and the Nizam's soldiers grow gaunt with thirst inside their own camp.

[warm] On the fourth day word comes that the Nizam has agreed to terms without one great battle fought at all, and a strange, quiet cheer runs down the picket line.

[reflective] Your captain says this is how Baji Rao wins wars: not by killing the most men, but by leaving the enemy nothing to fight for.

[tender] That night, for the first time since Rajgad, you sleep without your sword under your hand, and you dream of your mother's cooking fire instead of gunpowder.

## Chapter 4: The Seasons Between

[calm] The rains come, and with them the end of campaigning; even the Nizam's soldiers and yours agree, without a single treaty, to stop.

[warm] You ride home along muddy roads to a village that has aged half a season without you, and your mother's hands find your face before she says a word.

[calm] You spend the wet months mending fences and walking behind a plough instead of a horse, and you are surprised how easily your body remembers farming.

[reflective] The brass token from your father sits in a small box now beside a second one, given to you after Palkhed by an officer who never learned your full name.

[warm] Years pass this way: dry season riding, wet season farming, a shiledar's rank finally earned when you can afford your own horse and sword.

[sad] Not every season ends the same; one year the old trooper who taught you to grip the reins does not come home from the Konkan hills, and no one tells you exactly how.

[calm] You marry in a monsoon month, when the roads are too soft for any army to move, because even war leaves you these quiet windows.

[tender] Your wife learns to recognize the particular quiet you carry home after a hard campaign, and she never once asks you to describe the boy by the river.

[warm] Your own son is born between one season's fighting and the next, and you tie a thread of red cotton around his small wrist before he can even grip it.

[reflective] You tell him almost nothing of Palkhed for years, only that a good rider wins by patience, not by anger.

## Chapter 5: The Elder's Fire

[calm] Grey now, you sit most evenings by your own fire instead of a campaign one, listening to younger men argue about horses the way you once did.

[warm] Your son rides a shiledar's horse of his own these days, and when he leaves each dry season you press a small brass token into his palm without a word.

[reflective] You have outlived the old trooper, two horses, and more battles than you ever admit to counting, but the smell of woodsmoke and wet wool still calls Palkhed back to you.

[awe] Sometimes at night you still feel the exact moment your horse steadied under you at the river, cannon smoke drifting past like a held breath.

[tender] Your grandchildren ask if you were ever afraid, and you tell them the truth: only once, by a river, over a boy you never knew.

[calm] You tell them the rest too — the bhakri baked on stone, the goddess's name murmured at the fires, the discipline that let farmers become an army the Mughals could never quite corner.

[reflective] A rider still passes through the village each dry season, calling for young men the way one once called for you, and you watch your grandson's shoulders straighten at the sound.

[warm] You do not know if he will ride as a bargir or, one day, as a shiledar with a horse of his own, only that the thread of red cotton is already tied and waiting.

[calm] The wind moves through the millet fields the same way it moved through Palkhed's grass, patient and unhurried, exactly as your first captain said it would be.

[tender] And when your eyes close by the fire at last, it is not the cannon you hear, but your mother's hands, and the sound of a horse breathing quietly in the dark.
