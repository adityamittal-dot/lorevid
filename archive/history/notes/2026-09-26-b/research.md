# Research — Your Life as an Ottoman Janissary (2026-09-26, RESERVE slot b)

1. The devshirme was the Ottoman practice of forcibly recruiting boys from among their Balkan Christian subjects and raising them Muslim; the practice is first attested under Bayezid I around 1395 and again under Murad II around 1430. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devshirme
2. Ottoman officials, the "scouts," toured Balkan villages roughly every three to five years, and a decree ordered fathers to bring forward boys of a set age range so the best could be chosen — healthy, strong and handsome boys were preferred. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devshirme
3. Turkish historians estimate that, over the centuries the devshirme ran, around two hundred thousand boys were taken this way; the system supplied not only the Janissary infantry but also many of the empire's top administrators, including several grand viziers (debated exact total). — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devshirme
4. After selection, boys were marched to Istanbul in groups and then handed over to Turkish farming families in Anatolia for several years, to learn the Turkish language and adjust to Muslim life before being sorted further. — https://albanianregistry.org/blog/janissaries
5. The sharpest, tallest and most physically able boys ("inner boys," iç oğlanları) were chosen for the Enderun, the elite palace school inside Topkapı Palace, where they studied writing, Islamic law, and — depending on aptitude — calligraphy, literature, accounting or the arts of governing. — https://topkapipalace.com.tr/article/the-heart-of-the-palace/
6. The rest entered the Acemi Oğlan (cadet) barracks, a preparatory school where recruits learned Turkish, the fundamentals of Islam, reading and writing and basic discipline while officers quietly noted which boys showed talent, intelligence or character. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enderun_School
7. Full Janissary training was described as monastic in its intensity: years of drilling with arquebus, sword, bow and sabre, constant physical conditioning, wrestling and even archery on horseback. — https://timelessmyths.com/stories/janissary/
8. The Janissary corps as a whole was called the Ocak, meaning "hearth," and its officer titles were drawn straight from the kitchen: a çorbacı ("soup-man") commanded a company (orta), assisted by a sekbanbaşı ("head of the hunting-hounds"), with a sakkabaşı ("head water-carrier") and an aşçıbaşı ("master cook") among the non-commissioned ranks. — https://militaryhistorynow.com/2017/03/24/the-fighting-messmates-the-curious-role-of-food-in-turkeys-legendary-janissary-corps/
9. The emblem of the whole corps was a great cooking cauldron, the kazan-ı şerif ("honorable cauldron"); eating from the sultan's kazan was treated as a sign of loyalty and helped forge what soldiers called the "brotherhood of the spoon" among men who ate, slept, drilled and eventually fought side by side. — https://militaryhistorynow.com/2017/03/24/the-fighting-messmates-the-curious-role-of-food-in-turkeys-legendary-janissary-corps/
10. A Janissary company's greatest disgrace was letting its own cauldron be captured by an enemy; their signal for mutiny, conversely, was to overturn the great cooking pots in the barracks yard. — https://www.hurriyetdailynews.com/opinion/aylin-oney-tan/the-janissary-the-coup-and-the-cauldron-102015
11. Every fifteenth day of Ramadan the sultan gave the Janissaries trays of baklava in a ceremony called the Baklava Alayı ("Baklava Procession"): one large tray for every ten men, which they slung from a pole between two soldiers and carried back to barracks — a rare sweetness in an otherwise plain, disciplined diet. — https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/what-did-janissaries-eat
12. New Janissary recruits wore a distinctive tall white felt cap called the börk, with a hanging flap at the back; one popular legend ties its shape to the plain felt hat, or the trailing sleeve, of the wandering Sufi mystic Hacı Bektaş Veli (historians note the timeline is questionable, since he lived roughly a century before the corps existed) (debated origin). — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janissary
13. Soon after the corps was founded it adopted the Bektashi Sufi order as its spiritual home, regarding Hacı Bektaş Veli as its patron saint; the order's dervishes effectively served as the Janissaries' chaplains. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bektashism
14. On promotion into a full regiment a Janissary earned a base salary paid in silver akçe, plus a separate quarterly allowance for clothing and another allowance for weapons and ammunition — unlike ordinary slaves, they were salaried soldiers of the state. — https://military-history.fandom.com/wiki/Janissary
15. Infractions of discipline were commonly punished with the bastinado, a beating on the soles of the bare feet, administered in front of the company. — https://timelessmyths.com/stories/janissary/
16. For most of the corps's history Janissaries were forbidden to marry or take up an outside trade, so that their only loyalty and family was the Ocak; the marriage ban was gradually eased from the 1560s and formally lifted in the 1630s, after which men began enrolling their own sons and the once merit-based corps grew hereditary. — https://military-history.fandom.com/wiki/Janissary
17. Janissary arquebusiers were already part of the sultan's army under Murad II in the early 1400s, and by the siege of Constantinople in 1453 Janissary infantry — armed with bows, matchlock guns, scimitars and polearms — worked alongside Mehmed II's huge siege cannon, making them among the first standing infantry in the world built around firearms. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_matchlock_musket
18. The Ottoman military marching band, the mehter, is considered history's first recorded military marching band, dating to the thirteenth century; it played the davul drum, the zurna reed pipe and clashing cymbals, and its booming, driving sound was meant to carry over a battlefield and unsettle the enemy before a Janissary charge. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_military_band
19. Mehter music later crossed into Europe: composers including Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven wrote passages imitating its drums and cymbals, so the sound the Janissaries marched to still echoes in the concert hall. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_military_band
20. Janissary barracks stood at Istanbul's Aksaray district, close to Topkapı Palace, where hundreds of men lived, drilled and ate together in company halls built around each orta's own hearth and cauldron. — https://istanbultarihi.ist/470-the-place-of-soldiers-in-the-social-life-of-istanbul
21. Over the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the corps became so politically powerful that it could make or unmake sultans — most strikingly in 1622, when Janissaries and allied troops seized and killed the young Sultan Osman II after he tried to curtail their privileges (debated exact motives). — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auspicious_Incident
22. By the early nineteenth century the corps, some 135,000 strong on paper, had grown resistant to any modernization of the army; when Sultan Mahmud II moved to form new Western-style troops in 1826, the Janissaries mutinied. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auspicious_Incident
23. Mahmud II answered by turning artillery directly on the Janissary barracks; in the "Auspicious Incident" of 15 June 1826 the corps was destroyed as a fighting force, with six thousand or more men killed and many others executed, exiled or imprisoned in the aftermath (debated precise death toll). — https://www.britannica.com/topic/Auspicious-Incident
24. A new, Western-style corps, the Asakir-i Mansure-i Muhammediye ("The Victorious Soldiers of Muhammad"), was raised to replace the abolished Janissaries, and the mehter bands fell silent along with them until a ceremonial revival in the early twentieth century. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_military_band
