# Your Life as an Aztec Merchant in Tenochtitlan — Draft 1

## Chapter One: The Weight You Learn to Carry

1. Before the sun rises over Lake Texcoco, your mother folds a plain maguey cloak into your arms, and tells you the road will decide your whole life.
2. You are ten years old, and this morning your father straps your first bundle onto your back, to see how long you can walk before setting it down.
3. Your legs shake by midday, but you do not stop, because a porter who stops becomes a porter no one trusts with real cargo again.
4. At home your sisters grind maize into dough before dawn, and the slap of tortillas against the griddle is the first sound you remember each morning.
5. Your family belongs to the pochteca, traders bound by blood and guild, and no outsider may simply decide one day to join your calpulli.
6. Your father's canoe glides out each dawn along the canals that cut through the floating gardens, where your family's beans and squash still grow.
7. By twelve you can carry a bundle as heavy as a grown man, hour after hour, your shoulders numb, your sandals worn thin against packed earth.
8. Your grandfather tells you the pochteca answer to their own god, Yacatecuhtli, lord of the road, and that his favor is the only shield you carry.
9. On feast nights you watch traders tie their walking canes into a bundle and mark them with a drop of their own blood, praying for safe roads.
10. You practice counting cacao beans until you can weigh a whole trade in your palm, never once needing to spill a single bean onto the ground.
11. The year you turn fourteen, your father finally says the word you have waited to hear since you first stood under a full pack: ready.

## Chapter Two: The Night You Leave Home

12. You leave Tenochtitlan the way every pochteca leaves it, in darkness, long after the temple fires have burned down to a dull red glow.
13. Your own countrymen are the danger tonight, not strangers, so you wear the plainest cloak you own, and let your gold ride hidden inside a hollow cane.
14. The causeway stretches ahead of you, pale under starlight, and somewhere behind you the city you were born in disappears into black water.
15. Porters walk in front of you and behind you, each one carrying a bundle taller than himself, the strap pressed flat against his own forehead.
16. Nobody speaks above a whisper until the lake is far behind, because sound carries strangely over water, and travels farther than you would think.
17. By your third day walking, your feet have blistered and healed and blistered again, and the road has stopped feeling like something that will end.
18. You trade cacao beans for cotton in one town, cotton for salt in another, learning that every village prices the same goods differently by dusk.
19. An old trader teaches you to read a market before you enter it, watching which stalls the local buyers avoid before you set down a bundle.
20. At night you sleep in a circle with the other porters, your bundle as your pillow, listening for footsteps that do not belong to your party.
21. You begin noticing things nobody taught you to notice: how many warriors guard a gate, how full a granary looks, how nervous a chief seems.
22. Your uncle tells you, quietly, that some pochteca learn a foreign tongue and vanish for months, and that no one in the guild ever asks where.
23. You understand, slowly, that the roads you are memorizing are not only for trading, and that the emperor's eyes travel wherever his merchants do.

## Chapter Three: The Road No One Sees

24. Deep in unfamiliar hill country, your party is smaller than it should be, and the porters ahead of you keep glancing toward the tree line.
25. A group of local traders blocks the path at a narrow crossing, and for a long moment nobody on either side moves or speaks a single word.
26. Your guide sets down his bundle slowly, palms open, and offers the first gift himself, because the first gift decides whether this ends in trade or in blood.
27. Cacao beans change hands, then quetzal feathers, then a long careful silence, until at last the strangers step aside and let your party pass.
28. That night you cannot sleep, listening to your own heartbeat, understanding for the first time why a dead pochteca is buried with a warrior's honors.
29. In a foreign market you overhear two chiefs arguing about a border, in a language you have quietly learned to understand without ever admitting that you can.
30. You memorize every word without moving your face, because a pochteca's memory is the only ledger that no thief on the road can steal from him.
31. Word reaches your party that traders from Tenochtitlan were killed on a road not far from here, and every man around your fire goes still.
32. You think of your mother folding that first cloak into your arms, and you understand, fully, what the road actually costs a family.
33. You carry the news home yourself, walking faster than you have ever walked, because information is the one cargo that spoils if it arrives too late.
34. When you finally see the temple fires of Tenochtitlan again, something in your chest loosens that you did not know had been clenched for weeks.
35. You do not walk straight through the gates, even now, even after everything; you wait for full dark, because old habits kept you alive on the road.

## Chapter Four: The Merchant Who Hides His Gold

36. You are a grown trader now, trusted with your own bundles, your own porters, your own small share of the guild's long memory of every road.
37. At the great market of Tlatelolco, sixty thousand people move between rows so exact a stranger could find any good blindfolded, by smell alone.
38. Judges sit in a pavilion at the market's center, ready to punish any trader caught cheating a measure, and you have seen a man's goods destroyed for it.
39. You weigh cacao in graded boxes divided into twelfths, so precise that a buyer never once has reason to argue with what your hands have counted.
40. The smell of roasting chiles drifts between the cloth stalls and the feather stalls, and somewhere, faintly, a flute plays for no one in particular.
41. You have grown wealthy enough to dress like a noble every day, and still you choose the plain cloak, out of habit, out of law, out of care.
42. When a venture succeeds, you do not parade it through the streets; you hold a quiet feast for your own calpulli, doors closed, voices low.
43. Your daughter learns to grind maize the way your sisters once did, patting the dough round and even, and you watch her hands and see your mother's.
44. Your son is ten now, and one morning you strap a small bundle to his back, and watch his legs shake by midday the way yours once did.
45. You tell him what your grandfather told you: a pochteca's real wealth is not the gold hidden in his cane, but the roads he has survived.
46. You do not tell him yet about the crossing in the hill country, or the news you once carried home faster than your own fear could catch you.
47. There will be time for that, you decide, on some other night, when he is old enough to carry more than his own weight in worry.
48. For now you only watch him lift the bundle again without being asked, the way you once did, and something in you goes quiet with pride.

## Chapter Five: The Walk Home, One Last Time

49. You are older now, your knees slower on the causeway, your eyes still finding the North Star out of a habit thirty years of night roads gave you.
50. Younger traders ask you, before their first departure, what the road is really like, and you tell them only what you told yourself at their age: walk anyway.
51. You still leave the city in darkness, though you no longer truly need to, because the quiet has become a kind of prayer you are not ready to give up.
52. You think, sometimes, of the traders who never came home, whose names are spoken only at the guild's private feasts, remembered but never explained to outsiders.
53. You have walked farther than most nobles ever will, and still you fold your cloak the way your mother folded it, plain side facing out.
54. On this last long journey, your son walks beside you instead of behind you, and you let him choose which road to take at the fork.
55. He chooses correctly, the safer path, the one that costs a day but nothing else, and you feel the whole weight of your teaching settle into place.
56. When Tenochtitlan appears again across the water, its temple fires small and steady, you feel the same loosening in your chest you felt as a young man.
57. You cross the causeway together in the dark, unremarkable, unguarded, two plain cloaks among a hundred other plain cloaks moving quietly home.
58. At your own door, your wife has kept the fire low and the food warm, not knowing exactly which night, at last, you would return.
59. You set down your bundle for what you quietly suspect may be the last time, and your shoulders remember, gratefully, what it means to finally be still.
60. Your grandchildren will grow up hearing only that you were a trader, nothing more, and you decide, smiling, that this is exactly the story you wanted them to hear.
61. Long after the household sleeps, you sit a while longer by the low fire, listening to the lake outside, content at last to have nowhere left to walk.
