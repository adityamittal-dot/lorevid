# Research — Your Life as a Medieval Peasant Woman After the Black Death

Sources used (5+ distinct sites): thecollector.com, worldhistory.org, en.wikipedia.org, britannica.com,
historyskills.com, campop.geog.cam.ac.uk (University of Cambridge), thehistoryofengland.co.uk,
historymedieval.com, historyrise.com, british-history.ac.uk.

1. The Black Death (1348-49) killed roughly one-third of Europe's population and about 30-40% of the population of Britain. — https://history.washu.edu/news/how-black-death-made-life-better
2. The sudden labour shortage after the plague meant surviving peasants could demand higher wages and take on empty land that had belonged to dead neighbours, sharply improving their bargaining position. — https://history.washu.edu/news/how-black-death-made-life-better
3. Alarmed landowners had the Crown pass the Ordinance of Labourers (1349) and then the Statute of Labourers (1351), which tried to freeze wages at pre-plague levels and force idle workers into long-term contracts with the first employer who demanded it. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_of_Labourers_1351
4. Enforcement of the Statute relied on special royal commissions, and by the 1350s thousands of prosecutions were recorded against both workers who asked for higher pay and employers who paid it. — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/ehr.13355
5. Despite the law, real wages did not immediately rise after the Black Death, and resentment over the wage freeze simmered for decades (debated: historians differ on exactly how effective the Statute's enforcement remained by the 1370s). — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/ehr.13355
6. That resentment, combined with a new poll tax (the third since 1377) collected regardless of a household's wealth, was the immediate spark for the Peasants' Revolt of 1381. — https://www.britannica.com/event/Peasants-Revolt
7. The revolt's trigger was a royal official, John Bampton, trying to enforce unpaid poll-tax collection at Brentwood in Essex on 30 May 1381; the confrontation spread across southeast England within days. — https://www.britannica.com/event/Peasants-Revolt
8. Rebels led by Wat Tyler, inspired by the preaching of the excommunicated priest John Ball, marched on London and put demands to the fourteen-year-old King Richard II, including an end to serfdom and the wage cap. — https://www.britannica.com/event/Peasants-Revolt
9. Tyler was killed and the revolt collapsed within weeks, and the king went back on his promises, but the Crown never again tried to levy a poll tax on the poor in the same way. — https://www.britannica.com/event/Peasants-Revolt
10. A peasant woman's day centred on the household: cooking, brewing ale, spinning and weaving, tending a kitchen garden, milking, and helping in the fields at planting and harvest alongside her husband. — https://www.thecollector.com/day-life-medieval-women-england/
11. Milk was used mostly to make cheese and butter, and only occasionally drunk fresh; meat was rare on a peasant table since hunting was reserved for the lord, so eggs and fish were the more common protein. — https://www.thecollector.com/day-life-medieval-women-england/
12. Ordinary peasant clothing was grey or dark, undyed or cheaply dyed wool: a long shirt or sleeveless robe, a skirt, a cloak, a head-covering veil or coif, wool stockings, and wooden-soled shoes or clogs. — https://schoolhistory.co.uk/notes/lifestyle-of-medieval-peasants/
13. Peasant houses were built of timber and wattle-and-daub (clay mixed with straw), centred on a single hearth, and in winter the family often shared the space with their animals for warmth. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peasant_homes_in_medieval_England
14. Bread and pottage — a thick, ever-changing stew of oats, barley or millet simmered with whatever vegetables were on hand, kept going over the fire for days at a time — formed the backbone of the peasant diet. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pottage
15. At Eynsham Abbey in Oxfordshire in 1284, a married labouring couple's daily allowance included a white loaf, a larger black loaf, a gallon and a half of ale, a dish from the monastery kitchen, and a dish of pottage. — https://thehistoryofengland.co.uk/resource/medieval-prices-and-wages/
16. The Assize of Bread and Ale, in force from the thirteenth century, was the first law in English history to fix the price, weight and quality of bread and beer sold in towns and villages. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assize_of_Bread_and_Ale
17. Manorial records from villages such as Cuxham in Oxfordshire and Halesowen in Worcestershire — among the most complete manor court rolls to survive — record everyday disputes over debt, land and labour going before the lord's court. — https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/law-and-history-review/article/abs/manor-court-procedures-debt-litigation-levels-and-rural-credit-provision-in-england-c1290c1380/8F76CF89465397C4DDBB8E92E19DB92A
18. An unfree peasant woman needed her lord's permission to marry, and her family typically paid a fee called merchet for that permission; a further fine, legerwite, was levied on a woman (not the man) for premarital sex, and chidewite for bearing a child outside marriage. — https://www.nottingham.ac.uk/manuscriptsandspecialcollections/learning/medievalwomen/theme5/marriagearrangements.aspx
19. A dowry — cloth, pots, linen, land, or farm animals — was expected even from the poorest families, and without one a daughter usually could not marry; a father who disapproved of a match could withdraw the promised dowry. — https://www.nottingham.ac.uk/manuscriptsandspecialcollections/learning/medievalwomen/theme5/marriagearrangements.aspx
20. Peasants typically married later than nobles, often in their twenties, partly because it took time to gather enough for a dowry or to be established enough to support a household. — https://www.nottingham.ac.uk/manuscriptsandspecialcollections/learning/medievalwomen/theme5/marriagearrangements.aspx
21. Childbirth was a recurring life-threatening event: obstructed labour, haemorrhage and infection could kill mother or child, and labour could stretch on for days (debated: mortality estimates vary widely by source and status — one Cambridge study of elite women found rates as low as 1.2%, far lower than the popular assumption). — https://www.campop.geog.cam.ac.uk/blog/2024/09/19/childbirth-in-the-past/
22. Life expectancy at birth in England, calculated from royal records, fell from about 35 years in 1276 to about 29 years during the Great Famine of 1315-17, and to only about 17 years during the plague years of 1348-75 — though these figures skew young because they include heavy infant and child mortality. — https://historyrise.com/article/medieval-people-didnt-just-die-young-the-truth-about-life-expectancy/
23. Village life was governed by sound and season: church bells calling the hours and Mass, the creak of cartwheels, the thwack of the threshing flail, geese hissing in the yard, and the cries of peddlers and tinkers passing through. — https://historyisboring.com/castle-life/medieval-superstitions/
24. Villagers were nominally orthodox Catholic but freely blended it with folk belief — reciting the Latin Our Father and Hail Mary as much like a protective charm as a prayer, and keeping small rituals against ill luck or for a good harvest. — https://historyisboring.com/castle-life/medieval-superstitions/
25. The Church's liturgical calendar — feast days, fasts, Sunday worship — structured the working year as firmly as the harvest did, with the church bell effectively governing the shape of each day. — https://historyisboring.com/castle-life/medieval-superstitions/
26. Daily agricultural tasks were organised by a reeve, an official (sometimes a villager himself) who supervised the manor's labour and reported to the lord's steward. — https://courses.lumenlearning.com/atd-herkimer-westerncivilization/chapter/daily-medieval-life/
27. Families held their own strips of land in the open fields but cooperated on shared tasks like ploughing and haymaking, and owed labour service (or, increasingly after the plague, cash) to the lord in addition to working their own strips. — https://courses.lumenlearning.com/atd-herkimer-westerncivilization/chapter/daily-medieval-life/
