# Draft 1 — Your Life as a Medieval Peasant Woman After the Black Death

narrator_voice (working choice): bf_isabella · ambience (working choice): rain

## Chapter 1: The Empty Half of the Village
1. [urgent] By the time the bell rings for Nones, you will know whether your family's strip of land feeds four mouths this winter, or only three.
2. [calm] It is the spring after the great dying, and half the cottages on your lane stand with their doors open to the wind.
3. [sad] You are sixteen, and your mother is one of the ones the plague took, so the garden and the hens are yours to keep alive now.
4. [calm] Your father does not speak of the empty houses, but every dawn he walks past three of them on the way to the fields.
5. [calm] This year there is more land than there are hands to work it, and the reeve has stopped pretending he does not need you.
6. [calm] You are given a second strip of barley to tend, land that belonged to a family whose name no one says out loud anymore.
7. [calm] In the evenings you boil oats and dried peas into a pottage that thickens on the fire for three slow days before it is gone.
8. [warm] The bread is black rye, heavy and sour, but there is more of it this year than there was in your mother's best year.
9. [calm] At the well the other girls whisper that the lord's own steward rode through Cuxham asking why so many strips of land stand empty.
10. [reflective] Church bells still mark the hours the same as before the sickness, and on Sundays the same prayers rise, thinner now, over fewer heads.
11. [warm] Tonight the pottage is thick, the rye bread plentiful, and your father, for the first time in months, eats until he is finally full.
12. [calm] You do not know yet that this empty land beside your own will decide how you marry, and who you marry, before the year is out.

## Chapter 2: Merchet and a Marriage
13. [calm] By midsummer a young ploughman from the next strip of land has taken to walking the long way past your gate most mornings.
14. [calm] He holds two strips now instead of one, land freed by the same dying that widowed half the village, and it makes him a match worth weighing.
15. [calm] Your father counts out a dowry of one cooking pot, a length of grey wool, and a single young ewe, and still frets it is too thin.
16. [calm] Because you are unfree, tied to the manor as your parents were before you, the lord's steward must be paid before you may marry at all.
17. [calm] The fee is called merchet, and your father haggles over it at the manor court the way another man might haggle over a plough ox.
18. [tender] You stand at the back of the hall and listen to your own worth argued over in shillings, and you say nothing, because nothing is expected.
19. [warm] The wedding is small: a blessing at the church door, ale and pottage after, a ring of plaited rushes since no one can spare silver.
20. [warm] You leave your mother's hearth for his, carrying your dowry pot and the wool on your own back, the whole short walk between two lives.
21. [calm] His cottage is wattle and daub like your father's, one room, one hearth, and in the coldest months you will share it with the ewe.
22. [calm] You learn the rhythm of a household now yours to run: spinning by firelight, brewing ale every third day, milking before the light comes up.
23. [calm] Some mornings, walking the second strip of barley together, you catch him quietly doing the sums of how much more you hold than his father ever did.
24. [tense] But the manor court is already murmuring about wages frozen by a new statute from the king's own council, and neither of you yet knows what it will cost.

## Chapter 3: The Night the Midwife Was Sent For
25. [calm] Three winters on, the reeve reads a new order aloud at the church door: no labourer may take wages higher than before the plague.
26. [calm] Your husband spits into the frost once the reeve is gone, because the empty fields still need hands, and still the lord will not pay for them.
27. [calm] You keep your own counsel, because a woman who argues with the reeve is a woman remembered unkindly at the next manor court.
28. [warm] That spring you are carrying your first child, and your mother-in-law walks the lane with you each evening so you are never far from help.
29. [calm] The village midwife has buried three of her own children and still says the same steady words to every frightened girl: breathe, and let your body work.
30. [tense] Your pains begin at dusk in early autumn, and by midnight the midwife has sent a boy running for your husband, still out in the far field.
31. [urgent] The labour goes on through the turning of one candle into the next, and twice the midwife's steady voice is the only calm thing in the room.
32. [tense] Somewhere near dawn you stop being afraid of dying and start being afraid only of the silence that would follow if the child did not cry.
33. [awe] Then the cry comes, thin and furious and alive, and every woman in the room breathes out at once, as if they had been holding it together.
34. [tender] Your husband is let in at last, still smelling of the field, and he kneels by the straw and touches the baby's fist with one finger.
35. [reflective] You think, half-asleep, of your own mother, who is not here to see this, and for the first time in years you let yourself grieve her plainly.
36. [warm] By the time the child sleeps, you have already chosen her name, and quietly decided she will never once have to pay merchet to marry.

## Chapter 4: The Year the Roads Filled with Angry Men
37. [calm] Your daughter is walking and talking by the year the king's men come a third time asking for the poll tax, the same coin from every soul.
38. [calm] Your husband pays it for the four of you, then stands a long moment doing sums that will not come out right however he counts them.
39. [calm] Word comes from Essex, then from Kent, then closer, that villagers like you have stopped paying and started marching, led by men no grander than the reeve.
40. [tense] Two men from your own manor leave before dawn to join them, and for a week you do not know whether your husband will follow.
41. [calm] He does not go, in the end, choosing the child asleep by the hearth over whatever justice the roads to London might offer a peasant this year.
42. [calm] News reaches Cuxham in pieces: a great crowd in London, a boy king listening, a rebel leader named Tyler struck down before the whole crowd's eyes.
43. [calm] The poll tax collectors do not come again the following spring, though no one in the village dares call that a victory out loud.
44. [reflective] The statute that froze your wages is never formally undone, and yet somehow, strip by strip, year by year, it stops being enforced with any teeth.
45. [calm] What truly changes your life is quieter than any rebellion: there is simply, from now on, more land than there are peasants left to work it.
46. [warm] Your husband takes a third strip the following spring, and pays for it in coin instead of owed labour, which his own father never once managed.
47. [warm] You notice, hanging wool to dye by the door, that your daughter will grow up owning more of her own hands than you ever did.
48. [reflective] The bell still rings the same hours it always has, but the fields around it are wider now, and fewer of them belong to anyone but you.

## Chapter 5: An Ordinary Old Age
49. [calm] You are past fifty when your daughter marries, and for her dowry you give a cooking pot, good wool, and a ewe descended from your own.
50. [warm] No steward argues over merchet this time, because her intended is a free man now, bound to no lord for the right to wed.
51. [reflective] You have buried a husband, a mother, and one child too small to name properly, and you no longer flinch when you count them over.
52. [reflective] Some evenings you still walk past the three cottages empty the year you were sixteen, long rebuilt by families who never knew why the land was free.
53. [warm] You keep two strips of barley now instead of your mother's half of one, and a granddaughter follows you between the rows asking why everything is as it is.
54. [tender] You tell her about merchet, and pottage that cooked for three slow days, and a night the midwife sat with you until dawn, and she only half believes you.
55. [calm] The church bell rings the same hours it rang when you were a girl, and the same Latin words still rise thin over the same worn stones.
56. [warm] In the evenings now you sit by the fire mending wool you no longer need to sell, because there is, for the first time in your family's memory, enough.
57. [reflective] You think sometimes of the families the plague took whole, and you do not call what followed a blessing, only a strange, hard-won room to grow into.
58. [calm] Your granddaughter falls asleep against your side before the fire burns low, and you sit a while longer, listening to the wind find the gaps in the thatch.
59. [warm] Somewhere past midnight you bank the fire, glance once at the sleeping child, and think that whatever this life cost you, it was, in the end, entirely your own.
